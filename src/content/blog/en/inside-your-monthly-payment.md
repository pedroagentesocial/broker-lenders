Almost every buyer I work with does the same thing: they find a payment calculator online, type in the price and the rate, and get a number. Then they get a real Loan Estimate and the payment is $600 higher.

The calculator wasn't wrong. It just answered a smaller question. A mortgage payment has four parts — five if you have mortgage insurance — and the loan itself is only one of them.

## The two halves: the loan, and everything else

**Principal and interest** is the loan. On a 30-year fixed it never changes. Every payment splits between the two, and early on almost all of it is interest — that's amortization, and it's why extra principal payments in the first five years are worth so much more than in the last five.

**Taxes and insurance** are not the lender's money. They're collected from you monthly, held in an escrow account, and paid out on your behalf once or twice a year. The lender does this because a lapsed insurance policy or an unpaid tax bill puts their collateral at risk — but the practical effect for you is that a $4,000 tax bill never lands as a $4,000 surprise.

## Why the same house costs different amounts in different counties

Property tax rates are set locally, not nationally. Two identical houses an hour apart can differ by hundreds of dollars a month.

## Why your payment goes up in year two

This is the phone call I get every spring, and it surprises people who did nothing wrong.

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-title">At closing, your escrow account is funded with an estimate</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Nobody knows next year's tax bill yet, so the lender uses the current one — often based on the *previous* owner's assessed value.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-title">The county reassesses the property after the sale</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">If the previous owner had it for fifteen years, or had an exemption you don't get, the new assessed value can jump considerably.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">The part that stings</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Your escrow analysis arrives with a shortage</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">You now owe the difference for last year <em>and</em> a higher monthly amount going forward. The shortage is usually spread over twelve months, so the payment can jump twice as much as the tax increase alone.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">4</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-title">It stabilizes</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Once the shortage is paid off, the payment drops back to the new normal. Insurance premiums keep it moving a little every year after that.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>When I quote a payment, I look up what the taxes will be <em>after</em> reassessment, not what the seller is paying today. It makes my quote look worse than the one down the street. It also means my clients don't get a $250 surprise fourteen months after they move in. Ask any lender you're talking to whether their tax figure is the current bill or the reassessed estimate.</p>
</div>

## What is *not* in the payment

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Utilities</b> Power, gas, water, sewer, trash, internet. On a house this is commonly $250–$450 a month and it's rarely in anyone's budget.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>HOA dues</b> Paid directly to the association, not through escrow — but the lender <em>does</em> count them in your debt-to-income ratio.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Maintenance</b> A common planning figure is 1% of the home's value per year. On $400,000 that's $333 a month you should be setting aside.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Special assessments</b> Some areas add district or improvement assessments to the tax bill. They show up in the title work — read it.</span></div>
</div>

## The number to actually budget

Take the full payment from your Loan Estimate — not the principal-and-interest figure — and add utilities and a maintenance reserve. On the example above, that's roughly $3,136 plus $350 plus $333, or about $3,800 a month of true housing cost.
