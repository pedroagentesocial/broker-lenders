A baby on the way is one of the most common reasons people buy a house. It's also when a lot of buyers are about to go on leave, or already are, with a smaller paycheck or none at all for a few weeks. That doesn't have to stop the loan. Lenders have a specific rule for it.

The rules below are Fannie Mae's, which apply to most conventional loans. FHA, VA and USDA have their own versions, and lenders can add requirements on top.

## What counts as temporary leave

Fannie Mae describes temporary leave as usually short and started by you: maternity or parental leave, or a short-term medical disability, for example. Leave your employer imposes, like a furlough or a layoff, **doesn't** count, even if there's an expected return date.

If the employer confirms on the verification call that you're on temporary leave, the lender has to consider you employed.

Your first payment date is set at closing and is often about a month or more after it, so ask your lender for the exact date. Closing near the end of your leave can make the difference between qualifying on full pay or on reduced pay.

## The reserves top-up, with Fannie Mae's own numbers

If you'll still be on leave, the lender can add a supplement from your savings. It takes the liquid money you'll have left after the down payment, closing costs and required reserves, and divides it by the number of months from the first payment until your regular pay resumes, rounded up. That amount is added to your leave income, up to your regular pay.

Be upfront about the leave. The lender will verify your job close to closing, and the call will show it anyway.

## What a lender can't make you do

In 2014, HUD announced a $5 million settlement with Wells Fargo over allegations that it discriminated against women who were pregnant, had recently given birth or were on maternity leave, including by requiring them to return to work before their loans closed. HUD treats this as sex and familial status discrimination under the Fair Housing Act, and said it had received 190 maternity leave complaints against lenders since 2010.

The rule above exists so you don't have to cut your leave short. If a lender tells you to, ask it to show you where its guidelines require it.
