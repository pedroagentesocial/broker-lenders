Almost everyone gets a 30-year mortgage without thinking twice. Some people hear how much interest it costs and jump to a 15-year loan. Both can be the right call, but there's a third option that gets far less attention, and for many households it's the best of the three.

## The two loans, side by side

A $350,000 loan. Shorter terms usually come with lower rates, so this example uses 6.5% for 30 years and 5.75% for 15. Both rates are illustrative.

That's a remarkable saving. But the 15-year payment is a **requirement**. If you lose a job or have a baby, the $2,906 is still due. It also counts in full in your debt-to-income ratio, so it can reduce the house you qualify for.

## The third option: a 30-year loan with extra payments

With a 30-year loan you owe only the lower payment, and you can add to it whenever you're able. Each extra dollar goes to principal, which means you stop paying interest on that dollar for the rest of the loan.

And if you pay the 15-year amount on the 30-year loan? You'd finish in about 16.3 years and pay $218,361 in interest. That's **about $45,000 more** than the real 15-year loan, because the rate is higher.

So that's the real comparison. The $45,000 is the price of flexibility: the right to drop back to $2,212 in any month you need to.

That last point matters. Extra payments only save money if they happen. An automatic transfer does this better than good intentions.

## Biweekly plans: you don't need to pay for one

A biweekly plan means paying half your payment every two weeks. There are 26 half-payments in a year, which adds up to 13 full payments instead of 12. That's the entire trick.

You can do the same on your own. Adding one extra payment a year, about $184 a month on this loan, pays it off in about 24.2 years and saves around $101,800 in interest. If a company wants to charge you a fee to set up a biweekly plan, you don't need it.
