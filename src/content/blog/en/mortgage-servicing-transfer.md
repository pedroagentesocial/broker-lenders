A few months after closing, many homeowners get a letter saying their mortgage is moving to a company they've never heard of. It feels like something went wrong. Usually nothing did. Loans and the right to service them are bought and sold all the time, and federal rules say how it has to happen.

What matters is knowing what changes, what doesn't, and the few things you should check.

## Owner vs servicer

Two different companies can be involved, and they can change separately:

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th></th><th>What they do</th><th>What you get when it changes</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Servicer</td><td>Takes your payments, runs your escrow, sends statements and handles problems</td><td>A "goodbye" letter from the old servicer and a "welcome" letter from the new one</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Owner (investor)</td><td>Owns the loan itself</td><td>A notice from the new owner within 30 days of the sale</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The servicer is the one you deal with. A change of owner often doesn't change who you send your payment to.

## What doesn't change

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Your rate, balance and loan terms.</b> The transfer notice has to say that the transfer doesn't change the terms of your loan, other than those directly related to servicing.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Your due date and payment amount.</b> The amount can still change at your yearly escrow analysis, just like before.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Your escrow.</b> The balance moves with the loan, and the new servicer keeps paying your taxes and insurance.</span></div>
</div>

## The timeline

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">At least 15 days before</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">The old servicer's notice</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">It gives the effective date, contact details for both companies, and the date the old one stops accepting payments and the new one starts. Those have to be the same day or the next day.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Within 15 days after</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">The new servicer's notice</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Sometimes both companies send a single combined notice instead, at least 15 days before the transfer. Some transfers, for example after a servicer's bankruptcy, allow up to 30 days after.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">The first 60 days</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">The grace period</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">If you send a payment to the old servicer by mistake, and it arrives on time, the new servicer can't treat it as late for any purpose. That means no late fee for it. The old servicer has to forward the payment or send it back to you with the right address.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

## Your checklist

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Confirm the transfer is real.</b> Call your current servicer using the number on your monthly statement or their website, not the number in the letter.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Set up payments again.</b> Autopay through the old servicer or your bank's bill pay may not move on its own. Confirm with both companies and set up the new one before your next due date.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Tell your insurance company.</b> Your homeowners policy lists the servicer as "mortgagee." Ask your agent to update it so renewal bills go to the right place.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Check your first statement.</b> Compare the balance, the escrow balance and the payment with your last statement from the old company.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Keep proof.</b> Save both letters and a record of your last payment to the old servicer.</span></div>
</div>

## Watch out for fake transfer letters

Scammers send letters and emails that look like transfer notices, with a new address to send your payment. It's the same trick as [wire fraud at closing](/blog/closing-wire-fraud/): change where the money goes. A real transfer is announced by your current servicer, and you can check it with them. If the only proof is the letter itself, don't send money anywhere until you've called the number on your old statement.

## If something goes wrong

If the new servicer charges a late fee during the 60 days, misapplies a payment or makes a mistake with your escrow, send a written **notice of error**. Use the address the servicer has designated for errors, if it has one. In most cases the servicer has to acknowledge it within 5 business days and fix it or explain why within 30 business days. It can extend that by 15 days if it tells you why in writing.

<div class="bl-stats">
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">15 days</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Minimum notice from the old servicer before the transfer</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat is-accent"><div class="bl-stat-num">60 days</div><div class="bl-stat-label">On-time payments to the old servicer can't be treated as late</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">30 business days</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Usual deadline to answer a written notice of error</div></div>
</div>

If that doesn't solve it, you can file a complaint with the [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/). The company responds through the same system.
