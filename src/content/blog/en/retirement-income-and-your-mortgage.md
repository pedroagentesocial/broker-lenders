Retired, living on disability, or downsizing on a pension? You can still get a mortgage. Lenders don't ask whether you have a job. They ask whether your income is **stable, documented and likely to continue**, and many retirement benefits are among the most stable income there is.

The rules below are Fannie Mae's, which apply to most conventional loans. Other programs have similar rules with their own details.

## Your rights first

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act, through Regulation B, sets the ground rules:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Age</b> A lender generally can't take your age into account, as long as you're old enough to sign a contract. It can use age only to favor an applicant who's 62 or older, or, in judgmental reviews, as it relates to a pertinent element of creditworthiness.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Retirement income</b> A lender can't discount or exclude income because it comes from an annuity, a pension or another retirement benefit, or from part-time work. It has to evaluate that income individually.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Public assistance</b> A lender can't hold it against you that some of your income comes from a public assistance program.</span></div>
</div>

So "you're too old for a 30-year loan" is not a reason a lender can use.

## What counts, and what the lender needs

    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Social Security retirement or disability, on your own record</td><td>SSA award letter, SSA-1099, tax returns or proof of current receipt (one is enough)</td><td class="bl-yes">No, unless there's reason to think it will stop</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Social Security on someone else's record (spouse, ex-spouse, or a dependent child's benefit)</td><td>Award letter and proof of current receipt</td><td class="bl-no">Yes, from the application date</td></tr>

A few details worth knowing:

- **No minimum history** for Social Security or fixed pension payments. If your pension starts on or before your first mortgage payment, the lender needs a benefit statement showing the type, amount, frequency and start date.
- **Age can prove continuance.** When Social Security ties a benefit to age, verifying the borrower's age can satisfy the three-year rule. A child's benefit that ends at a set age in two years, though, won't count.
- **Retirement accounts can be combined.** To show three years of distributions, eligible balances in your 401(k), IRA and Keogh accounts can be added together, as long as you have unrestricted access without penalty.
- **Disability reviews aren't a red flag.** Fannie Mae says a periodic re-evaluation of benefits isn't a reason to think disability income will stop. The lender needs evidence you'll receive at least one payment by the first mortgage payment.

## The gross-up: why $1,500 can count as more

Lenders qualify you on gross income, before taxes. A paycheck loses part of that to taxes; nontaxable income doesn't. To make them comparable, Fannie Mae lets the lender add **25% of the nontaxable portion** to your income.

The same logic applies to other income the lender can verify is nontaxable, such as some disability benefits. The lender has to confirm it's nontaxable with an award letter, policy, statement or tax return.
