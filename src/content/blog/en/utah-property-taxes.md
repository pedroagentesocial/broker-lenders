Property taxes are one of the biggest parts of a Utah mortgage payment, and they're the part that changes most often. Utah also has a rule that cuts the tax on your home almost in half, as long as the county knows the home is your primary residence.

Here's how it works, and the dates that matter each year.

## The 45% exemption

Utah exempts **45% of the market value** of a home used as a primary residence. In other words, you're taxed on only 55% of what the county says the home is worth. A second home, a vacation cabin or a short-term rental is taxed on 100%.

A few rules worth knowing:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>One per household.</b> Only one primary residential exemption can be claimed per household in Utah. For this rule, a household is a married couple and their dependent children.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>It can apply to long-term rentals.</b> A home rented to a full-time tenant as their primary residence can qualify. The county may ask the owner for a signed declaration and proof, such as a lease.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>It covers up to one acre of land</b> with the home.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Answer the county's letter.</b> After a home changes owners, the county may ask you to sign a residential property declaration. If it asks and you miss the deadline, the home can lose the exemption for that year and be taxed on 100% of its value.</span></div>
</div>

Building a new home? There's a separate form (PT-24) to declare that a home under construction will be your primary residence. [Buying a new-construction home](/blog/buying-new-construction/) explains why the first tax bill on a new build can be misleading.

      <p class="bl-step-text">The county values your home as it was on January 1 of that year.</p>

## How to appeal

You can't appeal the tax rate. You can appeal the **value** the county put on your home. The question the board asks is simple: what was the home worth on January 1?

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Check the county's records first.</b> Square footage, bedrooms, a finished or unfinished basement, the lot size. A wrong detail is the easiest thing to fix.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Gather comparable sales.</b> Similar homes nearby that sold close to January 1. An appraisal from around that date also helps.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Document problems.</b> Photos and repair estimates for issues that lower the value.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Know the next step.</b> If you disagree with the county board's decision, you can appeal to the Utah State Tax Commission within 30 days of the decision.</span></div>
</div>

A higher value isn't always a reason to appeal. If most homes in your area went up, the tax rate may come down to compensate. Your notice shows what the estimated tax would be.

## Relief programs

Utah has several programs, each with its own requirements and applications, usually through the county:

      <tr><td>Veterans exemption</td><td>Veterans with a service-connected disability, scaled to the disability rating</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Active duty exemption</td><td>Military members after qualifying active duty outside Utah</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Homeowner's credit</td><td>Homeowners 66 or older, or surviving spouses, with limited household income</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Indigent abatement and deferral</td><td>Qualifying seniors, people with disabilities or owners in extreme hardship</td></tr>

The amounts and limits change, so check the [Tax Commission's relief page](https://tax.utah.gov/propertytax/tax-relief/) and your county assessor or treasurer for the current rules.
