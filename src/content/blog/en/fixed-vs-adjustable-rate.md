A fixed-rate mortgage gives you one promise: the principal and interest payment you sign for is the payment you'll make in year 1 and in year 30. An adjustable-rate mortgage — an ARM — trades part of that promise for a lower rate at the start.

Whether that trade is smart has nothing to do with predicting rates. It depends on one thing: **how long you'll keep this loan.**

## How an ARM actually works

Today's conventional ARMs have names like 5/6, 7/6 and 10/6. The first number is how many years the rate is fixed. The second means that, after that, the rate adjusts every **6 months**.

When it adjusts, the new rate isn't chosen by the lender. It's a formula written into your note: an **index** (for conforming loans, the 30-day average of SOFR, published by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York) plus a **margin** that never changes. Three caps limit how far it can move:

<div class="bl-stats">
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">2%</div><div class="bl-stat-label"><b>First adjustment.</b> The most a typical 5/6 ARM can rise the first time it resets. On 7/6 and 10/6 ARMs this cap is usually 5%.</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">1%</div><div class="bl-stat-label"><b>Each adjustment after.</b> The most it can rise every six months from then on.</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat is-accent"><div class="bl-stat-num">5%</div><div class="bl-stat-label"><b>Lifetime.</b> The most it can ever rise above your starting rate. This is your true worst case.</div></div>
</div>

Your exact caps, index and margin are on page 1 of your Loan Estimate, in the "Adjustable Interest Rate (AIR) Table" on the Closing Disclosure, and in the ARM disclosure your lender must give you. Read them before you choose.

The worst case isn't the forecast — the rate could just as easily reset lower. But it's the number you have to be able to survive, because it's the only one the contract guarantees. If a $3,474 payment would break your budget, the ARM's savings aren't savings; they're a bet.

  <p>The most common ARM mistake I see isn't the reset. It's "I'll just refinance before it adjusts." Maybe. But refinancing needs rates to cooperate, your income to still qualify, your credit to hold and your home value to hold — all on the same day, five years from now. Plan as if you'll keep the loan, and treat a refinance as a bonus.</p>

## The lender already stress-tests you

Because of that risk, you don't qualify on the teaser payment. For a 5/6 ARM, Fannie Mae has the lender qualify you at the **higher** of the start rate plus 2 points or the fully indexed rate. In the example above, that means your debt-to-income ratio is calculated as if the rate were already 7.50%. If the ARM is the only way the numbers work, that's information too.

## The question that decides it

  <div class="bl-versus-card is-a">

      <li>This is the home you plan to raise a family in or retire in</li>
      <li>Your budget has little room for a payment that rises</li>
      <li>Your income is steady, not growing quickly</li>
      <li>You'd rather never think about your mortgage again</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-versus-card is-b">
    <p class="bl-versus-head">An ARM can make sense if…</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">You'll move within the fixed period</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>A job relocation, a growing family or a planned upgrade is likely within 5–7 years</li>
      <li>You could absorb the worst-case payment, or pay the loan down hard</li>
      <li>It's a larger loan, where the rate gap is worth thousands a year</li>
      <li>You've read the caps, the index and the margin — and you understand them</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

If you're torn, a 7/6 or 10/6 ARM is the middle ground: less savings than a 5/6, but seven or ten years of a fixed payment, which covers most people's real time in a house.

## Before you decide

Ask your lender for a fixed quote and an ARM quote on the same day, and compare them on the same page of the Loan Estimate.
