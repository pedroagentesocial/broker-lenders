Somewhere in your closing papers there are two lines that start with "Title –". Most buyers sign past them. They're worth a few minutes, because one of them is the only thing in the whole transaction that protects your ownership of the home, and in Utah you often aren't the one paying for it.

## What title insurance protects against

The Utah Insurance Department puts it simply: title insurance protects against ownership problems that arose **before** you bought the property. Think of a previous owner's unpaid taxes, a lien from a contractor who never got paid, or a mistake in an old deed. The CFPB gives the same examples.

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Problems from the past</b> Claims against the home that existed before you bought it but weren't known at closing.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A defense, not just a check</b> If someone sues over a covered title problem, the policy requires the insurer to defend your title, not only to pay a loss.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Paid once</b> There's no monthly premium. You pay for it one time, at closing.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Not damage insurance</b> It doesn't cover fire, flood, theft or any other damage to the home. That's your <a href="/blog/homeowners-and-flood-insurance/">homeowners and flood insurance</a>.</span></div>
</div>

## Two policies, two different jobs

There are two kinds of title policy in a purchase, and they protect different people.

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th></th><th>Lender's policy</th><th>Owner's policy</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Who it protects</td><td>Your lender</td><td>You</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Amount of coverage</td><td>Up to the principal of your loan</td><td>The purchase price of the home</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Required?</td><td>Most lenders require it to make the loan</td><td>Usually optional; your Loan Estimate labels it "(optional)"</td></tr>
      <tr><td>How long it lasts</td><td>Until the mortgage is paid off</td><td>As long as you or your heirs have an interest in the home</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Who usually pays in Utah</td><td>The buyer</td><td>The seller, though it can be negotiated</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The point the CFPB keeps making is the one buyers miss: the lender's policy **does not protect your equity**. If a title claim comes up, it covers the lender's loan. Your down payment and everything the home gains in value are covered only by an owner's policy. The owner's policy usually costs more, because it's based on the full price instead of the loan amount.

## Who pays in Utah

According to the Utah Insurance Department, the seller usually pays for the buyer's owner's policy and the buyer usually pays for the lender's policy. "Usually" is the key word: it's a custom, and the buyer and seller can agree to something else in the purchase contract. If your offer asks the seller to cover other costs, read what it says about title before you sign it, so the owner's policy doesn't quietly become your expense.

The last point comes from federal law. Under the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, a seller may not require you, directly or indirectly, to buy title insurance from a particular company as a condition of the sale. Utah also requires title agents and escrow officers to hold a license from the Insurance Department, and you can look one up with the Department's licensee search.

## The title commitment: read Schedule B

Before closing, the title agency searches the public records, such as deeds, mortgages, wills, court judgments, tax records and liens, and issues a **title commitment**. It says the insurer is willing to issue a policy under certain conditions. It doesn't promise that there are no problems.

Boundary disputes with a neighbor are a common surprise: the Utah Insurance Department notes that title insurance doesn't cover them unless you buy additional coverage.

## Why your Loan Estimate shows a different price

This confuses almost everyone. Title companies usually offer a much lower "simultaneous" rate for the lender's policy when you buy both policies from them. Federal disclosure rules don't let the lender show that discount on the lender's line. The Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure always show the **full** lender's premium, and the owner's policy line is reduced to make the total come out right. The CFPB's own example:

This matters most when the seller is paying for the owner's policy. If the contract says the seller pays the full owner's premium, the seller's credit on your Closing Disclosure can be larger than the owner's line it appears to cover. The CFPB explains that the leftover credit can be shown against other title charges or as a separate seller credit. Nothing is wrong; it's just the way the form adds things up. Compare the **total** of the title lines, not each line by itself.

## When you refinance

The old lender's policy ends when the new loan pays off the old one, so a refinance usually needs a new lender's policy. Your owner's policy doesn't need to be bought again. It stays in effect as long as you own the home. Keep your copy: the Utah Insurance Department doesn't keep copies of title policies, so if you lose yours you'll have to ask the title agency that handled your closing.
