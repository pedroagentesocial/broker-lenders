In a divorce, the house is often the biggest asset and the biggest debt at the same time. And there's one thing many people don't learn until it's too late: **the divorce decree and the mortgage are two different contracts.** A judge can decide who gets the house. Only the lender can decide who owes the loan.

## Title vs the loan

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th></th><th>Title (the deed)</th><th>The mortgage (the loan)</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>What it is</td><td>Who owns the house</td><td>Who promised to repay the debt</td></tr>
      <tr><td>How a name comes off</td><td>A new deed, such as a quitclaim, as the decree requires</td><td>Paying off the loan, or the lender approving a release of liability</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Does the decree change it?</td><td class="bl-yes">It can order the transfer</td><td class="bl-no">No, the lender isn't a party to the divorce</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

Signing a quitclaim deed gives up your ownership, but if your name is still on the loan, you're still responsible for it.

## Three ways to handle the house

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Sell</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Sell it and split what's left</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">The loan is paid off at closing, both names come off, and each of you starts fresh. It's the cleanest option when neither spouse can afford the house alone.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Refinance</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">One spouse keeps it and refinances</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">The new loan is only in the name of the spouse who stays. It pays off the old loan, and it can also pay the other spouse their share of the equity. The spouse who stays has to qualify alone, at today's rates.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Keep the loan</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">One spouse keeps it and keeps the existing loan</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Useful when the current rate is low. Federal law protects a transfer to a spouse under a divorce decree, so the lender can't demand the full balance because of it. But the departing spouse stays on the loan unless the lender agrees to release them.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

With option 3, the spouse who stays can ask the servicer about an **assumption with release of liability**. For a Fannie Mae loan, releasing the departing spouse requires the servicer to confirm that the remaining borrower's credit and finances are acceptable. A 2024 CFPB report notes that for some FHA loans, after a divorce, an assumption can be done without a credit review if the person staying on title lives in the home and has made the payments for at least six months. Ask the servicer which rules apply to your loan.

Federal servicing rules also count a spouse who receives the home through a divorce decree as a **successor in interest**. Once the servicer confirms you, you can request account information and be evaluated for help with payments, as the borrower would.

With Fannie Mae, a buyout under a divorce or legal separation can be done as a **limited cash-out refinance** instead of a regular cash-out refinance. The main conditions:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>You owned the home together for at least 12 months</b> before the new loan closes.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Everyone signs a written agreement</b> that states the terms of the transfer and what happens to the money from the refinance.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The spouse who keeps the house qualifies alone</b> under the normal underwriting rules.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>The spouse who keeps the house can't receive any of the money from the refinance.</b> It goes to paying off the old loan, the buyout and the costs.</span></div>
</div>

## Buying again after the divorce

When you apply for your next mortgage, the lender looks at your divorce papers closely:

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th>Situation</th><th>How Fannie Mae guidelines treat it</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>You receive alimony or child support</td><td class="bl-yes">Can count as income with a decree or agreement, six months of receipt, and at least three more years of payments</td></tr>
      <tr><td>You pay alimony or child support</td><td>Counted as a monthly debt if it lasts more than ten more months. Alimony can instead be subtracted from your income</td></tr>
      <tr><td>The decree gave a debt to your ex</td><td>Doesn't have to be counted, even if your name is still on it</td></tr>
      <tr><td>You're still on the old mortgage but your ex got the house</td><td>Doesn't have to be counted if the lender documents the transfer of title</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

Voluntary or proposed payments without a written agreement don't count as income. And a one-time lump-sum equalization payment isn't treated as steady income. [The document list that gets you approved](/blog/documents-you-need/) covers the rest of what you'll need.
