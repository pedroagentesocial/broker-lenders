The advice you'll hear is "don't change jobs while you're buying a house." It's not wrong, but it's incomplete. Sometimes the better job shows up at the worst moment, or the move to a new city is the reason you're buying in the first place. What matters is which income the lender can count, and when.

The rules below are Fannie Mae's, which apply to most conventional loans. FHA, VA and USDA have their own versions, and lenders can add requirements on top.

## What lenders look for: a reliable pattern, not one employer

Fannie Mae asks the lender to check that your work history shows a **reliable pattern of employment over the most recent two years**. That doesn't mean two years at the same company. Frequent job changes are fine if you can still earn consistent and predictable income. A shorter history can work too, when other strengths in your file offset it.

## Why bonus and overtime may not count yet

Base pay is predictable. Bonus, commission, overtime and tips aren't, so Fannie Mae recommends a **two-year history** and accepts a shorter one only if it covers **at least 12 months** and other factors make up for it.

The lender averages the variable pay over at least 12 months. If it's going down, the lender has to confirm it has stabilized, and if it hasn't, the income isn't eligible.

## Gaps between jobs

Fannie Mae's rules ask the lender to look harder at your current job when there were gaps in the last 12 months. And when you have more than one source of income, there can't be any gap longer than one month in the most recent 12 months, unless the income is seasonal.

If you had a gap, expect to explain it. A short letter about why it happened (a move, school, caring for family) and proof you're back to steady work helps.

## The offer letter rule: qualifying on a job you haven't started

If you've accepted a new job but haven't started, Fannie Mae can still let you qualify on that income. Under one of its options, the conditions are:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A signed, non-contingent offer</b> It must name you, the employer, the position, the type and rate of pay and the start date, and show you accepted it.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A start date close to closing</b> No earlier than 30 days before the note date, and no later than 90 days after.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A purchase of a one-unit primary home</b> And you qualify on fixed base pay only.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Money to bridge the gap</b> On top of normal reserves, either six months of the new house payment, or enough resources to cover your debts and the new payment for the months until your start date, plus one.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Not for family businesses</b> The employer can't be a family member or anyone involved in the transaction.</span></div>
</div>

Any conditions in the offer, such as a background check, must be met before closing. A raise with your **current** employer can also count, if it's fixed base pay, fully verified and takes effect no later than 60 days after closing.

## The call days before closing

Even if you change nothing, your lender will check. For a job paying wages, Fannie Mae requires a verbal verification of employment no more than **10 business days** before the note date (120 calendar days for self-employment income). If your employer says you no longer work there, the lender has to sort that out before the loan moves forward.

That's why quitting, switching to part time or going from employee to contractor in the last weeks before closing can derail a loan that was already approved.
