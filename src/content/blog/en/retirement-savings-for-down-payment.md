For a lot of buyers, the biggest pile of savings they have is in a 401(k) or an IRA. Using it for a house is allowed. The question is which door you take the money out through, because a loan, a hardship withdrawal and an IRA withdrawal are taxed in very different ways.

This isn't tax advice. Your plan's own rules and your tax situation matter, so check both before you move any money.

## Door 1: a 401(k) loan

You borrow from your own account and pay it back, with interest, through your paycheck. The IRS limits are:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>How much</b> The lesser of $50,000 or the greater of $10,000 and 50% of your vested balance. With $60,000 vested, that's $30,000.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>How long</b> Normally 5 years, in substantially equal payments made at least quarterly. A loan used to buy your principal residence may be repaid over a longer period, if your plan allows it.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>If you stop paying</b> The unpaid balance becomes a deemed distribution: taxed as income, and possibly hit with the 10% tax. A deemed distribution can't be rolled over.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>If you leave your job</b> Many plans reduce your account by the unpaid loan (a plan loan offset). You can avoid the tax by rolling that amount into an IRA or another plan by your tax return due date, including extensions, for that year. That means coming up with the cash.</span></div>
</div>

The good news for your approval: Fannie Mae treats a loan secured by your 401(k) as a contingent liability. The lender doesn't have to count the payment in your debt-to-income ratio if it gets a copy of the loan document showing the account as collateral. The payroll deduction still comes out of your check, though, so budget for it.

## Door 2: a hardship withdrawal

Buying your principal residence is one of the IRS's safe-harbor reasons for a hardship distribution (mortgage payments aren't). But it's the most expensive door:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>It's taxed</b> Hardship distributions are subject to income tax, unless they come from Roth contributions. The 10% additional tax may also apply.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>It's permanent</b> You can't repay it to the plan or roll it over to an IRA.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>It's capped at the need</b> The amount is limited to what's necessary, and you can't reasonably get the money from another source.</span></div>
</div>

There's no first-home exception to the 10% tax for a 401(k). That exception is only for IRAs.

## Door 3: the IRA first-home exception

You can take up to **$10,000** out of an IRA to buy, build or rebuild a first home without the 10% early withdrawal tax. The conditions in IRS Publication 590-B:

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-title">$10,000 for life</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">It's a lifetime limit, not a yearly one. Earlier first-home withdrawals count toward it.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-title">"First-time" means no home for two years</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">You generally qualify if you had no present interest in a main home during the 2-year period ending on the date you acquire the new one. If you're married, your spouse must meet it too. The home can also be for your spouse, your child or grandchild, or a parent or other ancestor.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-title">Spend it within 120 days</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">The money has to go to qualified acquisition costs before the end of the 120th day after you receive it. If your closing slips, the clock keeps running.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

On a **traditional** IRA, the withdrawal is still income. The exception only removes the 10% tax. On a **Roth** IRA, your regular contributions come out first and aren't taxed. Earnings can come out tax-free as a qualified first-home distribution once 5 years have passed since your first Roth contribution, within the same $10,000 lifetime limit.

## How the lender sees your retirement account

Under Fannie Mae's rules, vested retirement funds are an acceptable source for the down payment, closing costs and reserves. The lender has to verify that the account is yours and vested. When the money is used for the down payment or closing costs, it needs proof you actually received the funds, so withdraw early enough that the deposit shows up in your bank statements with a clear paper trail.

For **reserves**, you don't have to take anything out. Fannie Mae doesn't require the funds to be withdrawn, and the lender has to confirm the account allows withdrawals regardless of your employment status. If you've borrowed against the account, the lender subtracts the loan from the balance it counts.
