The seller says yes, and within days you're asked to send money. That's the earnest money deposit, and for most buyers it's the first real dollars that go into the purchase. It isn't a fee and it isn't gone. Whether it ends up as part of your down payment or back in your account depends almost entirely on what the contract says.

## What earnest money is

The CFPB defines it as a deposit a buyer pays to show good faith on a signed contract to buy a home. Four things follow from that:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Someone else holds it</b> The seller or a third party, such as a real estate brokerage or a title company, keeps it until closing.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>It's your money at closing</b> When the sale closes, it's applied to your down payment or closing costs.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>It comes back if you cancel for an allowed reason</b> If the contract is ended for a reason it permits, the deposit goes back to you.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>You can lose it</b> If you don't hold up your end of the contract in good faith, it can be paid to the seller.</span></div>
</div>

## How much to put down

No federal rule sets the amount. It's negotiated in your offer, and local custom and how competitive the market is usually drive it. A bigger deposit can make an offer look stronger, but it's also more money at risk if you walk away for a reason the contract doesn't cover. Your lender cares too: Fannie Mae tells lenders to look closely at deposits that are large or above what's customary for the area.

## Contingencies are what protect it

The CFPB recommends making your offer contingent on getting financing and on a satisfactory inspection. With those conditions in the contract, if you can't get the loan or the inspection turns up serious problems, you aren't required to buy, and the deposit can come back to you.

The catch is the deadlines. Each condition usually has a date by which you have to act. If you let it pass and then back out, the protection may be gone. Put every deadline on your calendar the day the contract is signed, and talk to your agent before any of them.

## Building a new home

Builders often ask for their own upfront deposit, which is also earnest money. Before you pay it, the CFPB suggests asking the builder under what conditions it can be returned. Builders often work with a preferred lender, but you don't have to use it.

## What your lender will ask about it

Because the deposit is part of your money in the deal, the lender has to see where it came from.

If it isn't clear the money came out of your account, Fannie Mae has the lender ask for more proof of where it came from and that it moved from you to the seller, agent, escrow company or closing attorney. On an FHA loan, if the deposit came from a gift, the gift also has to meet FHA's gift rules.

## Where it shows up at closing

On your Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure, the deposit appears in the **Calculating Cash to Close** table as a negative number, because it's money you've already paid. It's subtracted from what you bring to closing.
