You made an offer, it was accepted, and three weeks later the appraisal arrives: the home is worth $20,000 less than you agreed to pay. It feels like the deal is dying. It usually isn't — but you need to understand exactly what changed, because the gap is bigger than it looks.

## Why a low appraisal costs more than the difference

Your lender doesn't lend on the price. It lends on the **lower of the price or the appraised value.** So when the value drops, your maximum loan drops with it, and the gap comes out of your pocket at full size.

  <p class="bl-fig-title">A $420,000 offer, appraised at $400,000</p>
  <p class="bl-fig-sub">Conventional loan with 5% down. The lender can finance up to 95% of the lower number.</p>
  <div class="bl-bars">

  </div>
  <p class="bl-fig-note">A $20,000 appraisal gap became $19,000 of extra cash at closing — on top of closing costs. Your loan amount fell from $399,000 to $380,000.</p>
</figure>

The good news: you have more leverage than you think, because the seller has the same problem. Any buyer with a mortgage will run into that same appraisal — and on an FHA loan, the appraisal stays attached to the property for months.

## Your five options

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Most common outcome</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Renegotiate the price</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Ask the seller to drop to the appraised value. With the appraisal in hand, it's not your opinion against theirs — it's a licensed appraiser's. At $400,000, you're back to 5% down: $20,000.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">The usual compromise</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Split the gap</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">At $410,000, you'd bring $30,000 — $10,000 above your original plan instead of $19,000. Many deals close exactly here.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Only if you truly want this house</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Cover the gap in cash</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">You keep the price and bring the extra $19,000. Be honest about it: you'd be paying above what the market supports today, and it can't come from a source the lender won't accept.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">4</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">When the appraisal has a real error</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Request a reconsideration of value</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">If the appraiser missed a better comparable sale, got the square footage wrong or ignored a renovation, your lender can submit a reconsideration of value (ROV). Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and FHA all have formal ROV processes. It works when you bring facts — closed sales, not listings — and rarely when you bring frustration.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">5</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">If your contract protects you</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Walk away</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">With an appraisal contingency, you can cancel and keep your earnest money. Read the deadline carefully: if it passes before you act, the protection can disappear.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

## FHA and VA buyers have built-in protection

Even if you waived the appraisal contingency, two programs protect you by rule:

<div class="bl-versus">
  <div class="bl-versus-card is-a">
    <p class="bl-versus-head">FHA</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">The amendatory clause</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>Required in every FHA purchase contract</li>
      <li>If the value comes in below the price, you aren't obligated to buy and you don't forfeit your earnest money</li>
      <li>You can still choose to go ahead and pay the difference</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-versus-card is-b">
    <p class="bl-versus-head">VA</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">The escape clause + Tidewater</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>The VA option clause works the same way: you aren't bound to pay more than VA's reasonable value</li>
      <li>Before finishing a low appraisal, the VA appraiser alerts the lender and gives a short window to send better comparable sales — the "Tidewater" process</li>
      <li>After that, a formal reconsideration of value is still available</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>You have a legal right to a copy of the appraisal — the lender must give it to you promptly, and no later than three business days before closing. Read it the day it arrives. The comparable sales on page 2 tell you whether you're looking at a fixable mistake or the market telling you something. In my experience, the second is more common, and listening to it is not losing — it's the appraisal doing its job.</p>
</div>

## How to lower the odds before you offer

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Ask your agent for the closed sales</b> Offers based on recent closed comparables appraise. Offers based on list prices and bidding wars are the ones that come in low.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Keep the appraisal contingency</b> If you're competing, shorten it or cap it ("buyer will cover up to $10,000 of any gap") instead of waiving it entirely.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Give the appraiser the facts</b> A one-page list of upgrades with dates and costs, handed over at the visit, is completely allowed — and often helps.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Don't try to influence the value</b> Nobody — not you, your agent, or your lender — may pressure an appraiser toward a number. Facts are fine; pressure isn't.</span></div>
</div>
