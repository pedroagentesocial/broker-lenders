A manufactured home is often the most affordable way to own a place of your own. But the financing works differently than for a site-built house, and the biggest decision isn't the rate. It's whether your loan is a **mortgage** on the home and the land, or a **chattel loan** on the home alone, more like a car loan.

## Mortgage or chattel: what the CFPB found

The CFPB studied manufactured home lending using national mortgage data from 2019. What it found is a good reason to slow down before you sign.

<div class="bl-stats">
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">≈ 42%</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Of manufactured home loans were chattel loans, secured by the home but not the land</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">60%+</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Of manufactured home borrowers owned the land under their home</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat is-accent"><div class="bl-stat-num">17%</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Of those landowners still took a chattel loan</div></div>
</div>

According to the CFPB, chattel loans generally have higher interest rates and fewer consumer protections than mortgages, and shorter terms. They're also harder to get. Of the applications to buy a manufactured home, about 42% were denied: 50% of chattel applications and 33% of mortgage applications, compared with 7% for site-built homes.

If you own the land, or are buying the home and the land together, it's worth asking whether you qualify for a mortgage before you accept a chattel loan.

## What makes a manufactured home eligible for an FHA mortgage

An FHA mortgage on a manufactured home (FHA's Title II program, the same one used for regular houses) has property requirements on top of the usual borrower rules. Under HUD's handbook, the home must:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Be built on or after June 15, 1976</b> under the federal manufactured home construction and safety standards, with the HUD Certification Label attached, or a label verification letter issued on HUD's behalf.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Have at least 400 square feet</b> and be designed as a one-family home.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Be classified as real estate.</b> It doesn't have to be taxed as real estate by the state.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Be built and remain on a permanent chassis,</b> and be designed for a permanent foundation built to HUD's Permanent Foundations Guide for Manufactured Housing.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Have gone straight to its site,</b> transported directly from the manufacturer or the dealer. A home that was already installed somewhere else generally won't meet this rule.</span></div>
</div>

The lender also needs a certification from an engineer or architect licensed in the state, confirming that the foundation meets HUD's guide. If the home has additions or alterations, the foundation certification has to cover them. The label and the foundation are the two things that most often delay these loans, so ask about both before you make an offer.

## Conventional: what Fannie Mae requires

Fannie Mae also buys manufactured home mortgages, with its own rules:

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th>Rule</th><th>Fannie Mae</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Home and land</td><td>Both must be legally classified as real property under state law</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Primary residence</td><td class="bl-yes">Single-width or multi-width</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Second home</td><td>Multi-width only</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Investment property</td><td class="bl-no">Not eligible</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Loan type</td><td>First mortgage only. Fixed rate, or an ARM with a 7- or 10-year initial fixed period</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Leased land</td><td>Only in a condo or PUD project approved through Fannie Mae's review, and you must own the home</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

In practice, if the home sits on a rented lot in a community, a conventional loan is usually off the table. [Conventional, FHA, VA, USDA or jumbo](/blog/loan-types-compared/) compares the programs in general.

## FHA Title I: the option for the home alone

FHA has a second, separate program for manufactured homes: **Title I**. Unlike the Title II mortgage, it can finance the home by itself, the home and the lot together, or just the lot. HUD currently lists these nationwide limits:

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th>Title I loan</th><th>Nationwide limit</th><th>Maximum term</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Home only, single-section</td><td>$105,532</td><td>20 years + 32 days</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Home only, multi-section</td><td>$193,719</td><td>20 years + 32 days</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Home and lot, single-section</td><td>$148,909</td><td>20 years + 32 days</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Home and lot, multi-section</td><td>$237,096</td><td>25 years + 32 days</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Lot only</td><td>$43,377</td><td>15 years + 32 days</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The actual loan can be lower than these limits, because HUD also applies a minimum cash investment, credit score limits and a maximum loan-to-value. Not every lender offers Title I, so ask directly.
