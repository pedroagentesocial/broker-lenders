"You'll get a big tax break" is one of the most repeated lines in home buying. For some buyers it's true. For many others, the mortgage interest deduction saves little or nothing, because it only helps if you itemize, and the standard deduction is already high.

This isn't tax advice, and your situation may be different. But you should know how it works before you count on a tax refund to make the payment fit.

## The rule that decides everything: itemize or not

Each year you choose between the **standard deduction** and **itemizing**: adding up specific expenses like mortgage interest, state and local taxes, and charitable gifts. You take whichever is bigger. Mortgage interest only lowers your taxes if, with it included, your itemized deductions beat the standard deduction.

For 2026, the IRS standard deduction is more than $16,000 for a single filer and more than $32,000 for a married couple filing jointly.

The single buyer gets a real benefit. The couple, with exactly the same house and loan, gets nothing from the mortgage interest, because the standard deduction already beats their itemized total.

## The limits on what counts

Even when you itemize, the IRS limits which interest you can deduct:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The first $750,000 of debt.</b> Interest on mortgages used to buy, build or substantially improve your home is deductible on up to $750,000 of debt ($375,000 if married filing separately). Loans taken out before December 16, 2017 keep the older $1 million limit.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Your main home and one second home.</b> The limit applies to the combined mortgages on both.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Home equity loans and HELOCs only count if the money went into the home.</b> Interest is deductible only to the extent the money was used to buy, build or substantially improve the home that secures the loan. Use it to pay off a car or credit cards and that interest isn't deductible.</span></div>
</div>

If you're weighing a HELOC against a cash-out refinance, keep that last rule in mind. [HELOC or cash-out refinance?](/blog/heloc-vs-cash-out-refinance/) compares them.

## Points: deductible now, or over time

Points, also called discount points, are money paid at closing to get a lower rate. [Rate locks, points and 2-1 buydowns](/blog/rate-locks-and-buydowns/) explains how they work. The tax treatment depends on the loan:

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th>Situation</th><th>How the points are usually deducted</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Buying your main home</td><td class="bl-yes">In full the year you pay them, if you meet the IRS tests</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Points the seller paid for you</td><td>Treated as if you paid them, but they lower your cost basis in the home</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Refinancing</td><td>Generally spread over the life of the loan</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Second home</td><td>Only over the life of the loan</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The IRS tests include that the points are shown clearly on your settlement statement as points, are figured as a percentage of the loan, and that the money you brought to closing, plus any points the seller paid, was at least as much as the points. Money borrowed from the lender doesn't count. If you refinance and spread the points over the loan, you can generally deduct whatever is left when the loan is paid off, unless you refinance with the same lender.

## Mortgage insurance is deductible again in 2026

The itemized deduction for mortgage insurance premiums expired for several years. The IRS draft of Publication 936 for 2026 says it has been **reinstated for tax year 2026**. It covers private mortgage insurance and the mortgage insurance from FHA, VA (the funding fee) and USDA (the guarantee fee), for policies issued after 2006 on a loan used to buy, build or improve a first or second home.

So it mostly helps moderate-income buyers who itemize. And like interest, it only matters if your itemized total beats the standard deduction. [Mortgage insurance, explained](/blog/mortgage-insurance-explained/) shows what it costs on each loan type.

## The paperwork

If you paid $600 or more of mortgage interest on a loan during the year, your servicer will generally send you **Form 1098**. It shows the interest, mortgage insurance premiums and, in the year you buy, the deductible points. Keep your closing statement too: it's the document that shows points, including any the seller paid.
