Mortgage insurance is the most resented line on a mortgage statement, and the most misunderstood. Two things people get wrong: it does not protect them, and — on a conventional loan — it is not permanent.

It protects the lender against the loss they'd take if you defaulted with little equity. In exchange, you get to buy a decade earlier than you otherwise could. That's the trade. Whether it's a good trade depends almost entirely on how long you'll be paying it.

## The four kinds, side by side

The upfront amounts on FHA and USDA are almost always financed into the loan rather than paid in cash — which is convenient, and also means you pay interest on them for thirty years.

That's the worst case, and it assumes your house is worth exactly what you paid and you never send an extra dollar. Neither is usually true.

## Three ways to get rid of it faster

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Costs nothing</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Pay extra principal</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">An extra $200 a month on the example above pulls the 80% date in by roughly three years. Every dollar of extra principal in the early years is doing double duty: less interest, and an earlier PMI exit.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Costs an appraisal</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Use appreciation — request cancellation on current value</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">This is the one nobody uses. Servicers generally allow cancellation based on the property's <em>current</em> value once the loan is seasoned — often two years at 75% loan-to-value, or five years at 80%. If your area appreciated, an appraisal costing a few hundred dollars can end a $200 monthly payment years early. Call your servicer and ask for their written requirements.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Costs closing costs</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Refinance out of it</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">For FHA borrowers under 10% down this is the <em>only</em> exit. Once you have 20% equity and a credit score in the 680s, refinancing from FHA to conventional drops the MIP entirely — and that saving alone often justifies the refinance even without a lower rate.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>Put a calendar reminder for two years after closing that says "call servicer about PMI." Nobody at the loan servicer is going to call you. The automatic termination at 78% is their obligation and they do honor it — but the earlier cancellation, the one based on what your house is worth today, only happens if you ask. I have seen that phone call save clients $4,000.</p>
</div>

## The VA funding fee, since it's different

VA loans have no monthly mortgage insurance at all. Instead there's a one-time funding fee, usually financed into the loan, and it's the reason VA financing is so hard to beat.

## The decision this all comes down to

If you'll be in the house five years, FHA's permanent MIP costs you five years of it and you refinance or sell before it matters much. If you'll be there twenty, the same MIP is a five-figure difference, and getting to conventional — now or later — is worth real effort.
