Many buyers think the appraisal already checked the house. It didn't. The appraisal tells the lender what the home is worth. The home inspection tells you what's wrong with it. They're different jobs, done for different people, and only one of them is optional.

That's exactly why some buyers skip it. Here is why you shouldn't, and how the timing works in Utah.

## The appraisal is not an inspection

FHA puts it in writing. Every FHA lender has to give you a HUD form called **"For Your Protection: Get a Home Inspection"** at first contact, whether that's a pre-qualification, a pre-approval or an application. The form says it plainly: an appraisal is required to estimate the home's value for your lender, and it does not replace a home inspection.

The same HUD form adds something worth reading twice: FHA doesn't guarantee the value or condition of your home. If you find problems after closing, FHA can't give or lend you money for repairs, and it can't buy the home back. If the appraisal comes in low, that's a different problem. [The appraisal came in low](/blog/low-appraisal/) covers your options.

## What an inspection covers

According to HUD, a qualified inspector evaluates the home's physical condition, estimates the remaining useful life of the major systems, equipment, structure and finishes, and lists what needs to be repaired or replaced. Ask the inspector for their standards of practice or a sample report before you hire them, so you know what's included.

The Utah purchase contract gives you a good checklist of what you're allowed to look into during due diligence:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The structure.</b> The roof, walls and foundation.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The systems.</b> Plumbing, electrical, mechanical, heating and air conditioning, and the appliances.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Health and environment.</b> Hazardous substances, lead-based paint, environmental issues and geologic conditions.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Insurance.</b> The cost and availability of homeowners insurance, and flood insurance if it applies.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The rest.</b> Water sources and quality, property lines, square footage, HOA dues, utility costs and any other matter that's important to you.</span></div>
</div>

Under the contract, you pay for your due diligence and you choose who does it. You also pay for any damage the inspections cause.

## Radon: the test most Utah buyers should add

A standard inspection doesn't always include radon. The HUD form says the EPA and the Surgeon General recommend testing all houses for radon, and suggests asking your inspector about other tests, such as mold, water quality, asbestos or pests.

<div class="bl-stats">
  <div class="bl-stat is-accent"><div class="bl-stat-num">1 in 3</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Utah homes tested with elevated radon, according to Utah DEQ (1 in 2 in some areas)</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">4 pCi/L</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Level at which the EPA says to fix the problem. It suggests considering a fix between 2 and 4</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">≈ 21,000</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Lung cancer deaths a year in the U.S. that the EPA attributes to radon</div></div>
</div>

You can't see or smell radon, so the only way to know is to test. Utah DEQ sells $10.95 test kits at radon.utah.gov and says mitigation systems usually cost around $1,500. If the test comes back high during your due diligence period, that's a cost you can negotiate with the seller.

## The Utah deadline that decides your earnest money

In Utah, real estate agents normally use the state-approved Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC). The current version took effect November 1, 2026. Three dates in it matter for the inspection:

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Within 4 calendar days of acceptance</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">You deliver the earnest money</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Your brokerage then has four calendar days to deposit it in its trust account, or with a title company if you sign that addendum.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Before the Due Diligence Deadline</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Inspect, then cancel or resolve in writing</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">If you don't like the results, you can cancel with written notice and the earnest money is released to you. Or you can reach a written agreement with the seller on repairs or a credit.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">If the deadline passes</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">The protection is gone</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">If you didn't cancel or resolve your objections in writing, you're treated as having waived the due diligence condition, and the earnest money becomes non-refundable, except under the appraisal and financing conditions.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

Two details people miss. First, the due diligence condition only applies if it's checked in the contract. Second, a phone call with the seller's agent doesn't count. Cancellations and agreements have to be in writing, before the deadline.

The contract also says you're buying the home as-is, and that if you decide to close after your due diligence, you're relying on your own judgment and that of the inspectors you hired. In other words, the inspection is your main protection.
