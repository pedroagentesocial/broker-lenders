Panels on the roof sound like a bonus: lower power bills from day one. Sometimes they are. But the panels may belong to a solar company, come with a monthly payment you'd take over, or be tied to a lien on the house. Under Fannie Mae's rules, which apply to most conventional loans, the answer to **who owns the panels** changes your appraisal, your debt ratio and even whether the loan can close.

## Owned outright: the simple case

If the seller paid cash, paid off the financing, or bought the panels as part of the house, they're just part of the home. Fannie Mae's standard appraisal, insurance and title requirements apply.

## Financed: look for a lien

Solar loans come in two forms, and the paperwork tells you which:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>A UCC fixture filing in the land records</b> The lender treats the panels as a fixture tied to the house. The debt counts in your debt ratio and in the combined loan-to-value. If there's a senior fixture filing, it has to be subordinated. The panels add value only if the documents say they can't be repossessed in a default.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A personal loan that's not on the title report</b> If the debt is yours, it counts in your debt ratio, and the panels get no value in the appraisal or the loan-to-value.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Ownership is unclear</b> No value can be given to the panels unless a UCC personal property search confirms no lender other than the mortgage lender claims them as collateral.</span></div>
</div>

Often the seller pays off the solar loan from the sale proceeds. If you're expected to take it over, treat it like any other debt you're adding.

## Leased or PPA: you're taking over a contract

With a lease you pay a fixed monthly amount to use the panels. With a PPA you pay for the electricity they produce. Either way the solar company owns them, so the panels can't be counted in the appraised value, even if there's a precautionary UCC filing.

The lender has to read the agreement. Fannie Mae requires that:

On the payment: a **lease** payment counts in your debt-to-income ratio unless the lease sets a fixed energy payment with a prorated production guarantee. A **PPA** payment based only on the energy produced can be left out.

## PACE loans: paid off at closing

Some panels are paid for with a PACE loan, which is repaid through the property tax bill. Fannie Mae won't buy a loan on a home with a PACE loan unless the PACE loan is **paid in full before or at closing**. If the seller has one, the payoff has to be part of the deal.

## About the tax credit

The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit was 30% of the cost of new qualified property. The IRS says it's not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, and used (previously owned) clean energy property isn't eligible. Don't count on a credit for panels that came with the house.
