Every year, the federal government sets a ceiling on how large a mortgage Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac can buy. Loans under that ceiling are **conforming**. Loans above it are **jumbo**, and they work differently.

It matters most in expensive markets, but as prices rise, more and more homes land close to the line.

The conforming limit rose $26,250 from 2025, about 3.26%, following home prices. Alaska, Hawaii, Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands have higher limits: a baseline of $1,249,125 and a maximum of $1,873,675 for one unit. Two- to four-unit homes have higher limits too.

Your county's limit can fall anywhere between the baseline and the maximum. FHFA publishes the conforming limit for every county, and HUD publishes FHA's.

## The limit is on the loan, not the price

This is the most common confusion. A $900,000 home isn't automatically a jumbo loan. What matters is how much you borrow.

## What changes with a jumbo loan

Jumbo loans aren't bought by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, so each lender sets its own rules. Common differences:

    <ul class="bl-versus-list">

      <li>Rules published by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac</li>

A jumbo isn't necessarily worse. Strong borrowers sometimes get excellent jumbo pricing. But you should compare offers carefully, because there's no single standard.

## FHA has its own limits by county

FHA's limits work like the conforming ones, but lower. In 2026, the floor is $541,287 for one unit, and in the most expensive counties it goes up to $1,249,125. If you're using FHA in a lower-cost county, you can hit FHA's limit well before you'd ever reach a jumbo loan. In that case a conventional loan may be the better route.
