A weekend job, a few shifts somewhere else or a summer of seasonal work can be the difference between the house you want and the one you can qualify for. Lenders can count that money. The catch is that they count **history**, not effort. Extra income you've had for a few months often doesn't count yet.

The rules below are Fannie Mae's, which apply to most conventional loans. FHA, VA and USDA have their own versions, and lenders can add requirements on top.

## The rule for more than one job

When you're qualifying with income from more than one job at the same time, each source has to meet the rules for its own type of pay, and Fannie Mae adds three more:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Two years is the target</b> Fannie Mae recommends a two-year history for each income source.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Twelve months is the floor</b> A shorter history can work only if it's at least 12 months and other strengths in your file offset it.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>No gap longer than one month</b> If your history includes different employers, you can't have a gap of more than one month in the most recent 12 months. Seasonal income is the exception, under its own rules.</span></div>
</div>

## Part-time and variable hours

Fannie Mae doesn't treat part-time as its own category. What matters is whether your hours are guaranteed.

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th>Type of pay</th><th>History needed</th><th>How it's calculated</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Fixed hourly pay with guaranteed minimum hours</td><td class="bl-yes">No minimum history</td><td>Hourly pay × average weekly hours × 52, divided by 12</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Hours that change, or an hourly rate that changes</td><td>At least 12 months</td><td>Averaged over at least the most recent 12 months</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Variable pay that's going down</td><td class="bl-no">It has to level off first</td><td>If it has stabilized, the lender uses this year's pay divided by the months elapsed. If not, it can't be used</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

So a 25-hour-a-week job with guaranteed hours can count from the first paystub, while a job where you pick up whatever shifts are available needs a year behind it.

## Seasonal work

Seasonal income, like construction, landscaping, ski resorts or holiday retail, needs a **two-year history**. The lender averages this year's earnings with the previous two years, and needs a verification of employment.

Unemployment benefits during the off season can count too, if they're clearly tied to seasonal layoffs, expected to recur and reported on your personal tax returns.

## Side business or gig work

If you own 25% or more of a business, Fannie Mae considers you self-employed for that income. That usually means two years of tax returns, although 12 months can work if your most recent signed returns show a full year from the current business and you earned the same or more in similar work before.

If your side work is paid on a 1099 rather than a W-2, there's no employer paystub, so expect your lender to look at your tax returns. Ask how it will be handled before you count on it.

## What to bring

For each job paid on a W-2: your most recent paystub, dated no earlier than 30 days before you apply and showing year-to-date earnings, plus W-2s for the last one or two years. If you've had a gap, a short letter explaining it.
