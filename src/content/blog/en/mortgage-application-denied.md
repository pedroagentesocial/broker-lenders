Hearing "no" on a mortgage feels final. Usually it isn't. A denial is one lender's answer to one application, and federal law makes that lender explain itself in writing. The explanation is the most useful thing you'll get out of the whole process, because it tells you exactly what to change.

The rules below come from the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and its Regulation B, plus the credit reporting rules the CFPB explains. They apply to every mortgage lender.

## When you should hear back

Once your application is complete, the lender has **30 days** to tell you whether it was approved, denied or met with a counteroffer. If your file is missing something, the lender can instead send a notice of incompleteness that says what it needs and gives you a reasonable time to send it.

A counteroffer isn't a denial. If the lender offers a smaller loan or different terms and you don't accept, the formal notice can come up to 90 days after the counteroffer.

## What the notice has to say

The denial has to be in writing and include:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The action taken</b> That your application was denied, plus the lender's name and address.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The specific reasons, or how to get them</b> If they aren't listed, you can ask within 60 days and the lender has 30 days to answer.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The credit score it used</b> If your credit report played a part, the number, the key factors that hurt it and the credit bureau's contact information.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Your right to a free credit report</b> From that bureau, if you ask within 60 days.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Your rights under federal law</b> A statement of the anti-discrimination rule and the agency that supervises the lender.</span></div>
</div>

The reasons have to be **specific**. "You didn't meet our internal standards" isn't enough under Regulation B. "Debt-to-income ratio too high" or "insufficient funds to close" is what a real reason looks like.

## You get the appraisal, even if you're denied

If the lender ordered an appraisal, you have a right to a copy, whether the loan was approved, denied or withdrawn. The lender can't charge you for the copy itself, though it can charge for the appraisal. If the loan won't close, the copy is due no later than 30 days after the lender decides that. Read it. If the value is the problem, it shows you why.

## If the credit report is wrong

Pull the free report the notice offers and read every account. If something is wrong, dispute it with the credit bureau and with the company that reported it, in writing and with copies of your proof. The company that reported it generally has to investigate within 30 days, and anything it can't verify has to be corrected or removed.

## If you think it was discrimination

Lenders can't deny you because of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status or age, because your income comes from public assistance, or because you exercised your credit rights in good faith. If you think that happened, you can file a complaint with the CFPB online or at (855) 411-2372.
