Most mortgages get paid off long before the last scheduled payment. You sell the house, you refinance, or you finally send the lump sum you've been saving. Each of those starts the same way: with a number that isn't the balance on your statement.

## Your payoff amount isn't your balance

The balance on your monthly statement is what you owed on the statement date. The **payoff amount** is what it takes to close the loan on a specific day. According to the CFPB, it includes the interest owed through the day you pay off, any unpaid fees and, if your loan has one, a prepayment penalty.

Because interest keeps adding up every day, a payoff figure is good only for the date on it. If the closing or your transfer slips, ask for a new one.

## The servicer has seven business days

For any mortgage on a home, the servicer has to send you an accurate payoff statement **within a reasonable time and no more than seven business days** after a written request from you or someone acting for you. That's how a title company handling a sale or refinance gets it. The deadline can be longer if the loan is in bankruptcy or foreclosure, is a reverse mortgage, or the area was hit by a natural disaster.

## Does your loan have a prepayment penalty?

Most don't, but check before you assume. The FTC suggests looking at your monthly statement and your closing papers, typically the note or an addendum. ## Your escrow money comes back

If your taxes and insurance were paid through escrow, there's usually money left in the account when the loan ends. The servicer has to refund it **within 20 days, not counting weekends and federal holidays**, after you pay the loan in full.

If you're refinancing with the same servicer, it can instead move that balance into the new loan's escrow account, but only if you agree.
