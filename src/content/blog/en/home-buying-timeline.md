The offer gets accepted and everything goes quiet. For most buyers, the next four weeks feel like nothing is happening and they're the only one worried about it.

Plenty is happening. Here is the whole thing on one page, so you know what's normal and what's a problem.

## Week by week

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Days 0–7 · Your busiest week</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Contract, earnest money, inspection, disclosures</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">You wire earnest money to the title company (never to an email address you weren't given by phone). You schedule the home inspection. Your lender sends the Loan Estimate and an initial document list. Everything you send this week is a day you buy back later.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Days 7–14</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Inspection negotiation and appraisal ordered</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">The inspection report comes back with a list — every house has one. You negotiate repairs or a credit. Once your inspection period passes, the appraisal is ordered. Most lenders wait, because an appraisal is money you don't get back if you walk.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Days 14–28 · The quiet weeks</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Appraisal returns, underwriting sends conditions</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">The underwriter reviews everything and issues conditions: a letter explaining a deposit, an updated pay stub, proof a collection was paid. This is normal — conditions are not rejections. Answer them the same day and you stay on schedule.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">4</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Days 28–38</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Clear to close, then the Closing Disclosure</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">"Clear to close" means underwriting is done. Your Closing Disclosure must be in your hands at least three business days before you sign — that window is federal law and it cannot be waived for convenience. Read it against your Loan Estimate.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">5</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Days 38–45</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Final walkthrough, signing, funding, recording</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Walk the house the day before or the morning of. Then you sign, the lender wires funds, the deed records with the county, and you get keys. In most states recording and keys happen the same day; in some, the next.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>The single most dangerous moment in this whole timeline is the wire. Wire fraud in real estate is real, it targets buyers in exactly this window, and the emails look perfect. Before you send a dollar, call the title company at the number you looked up yourself — not the one in the email — and verify the instructions out loud. If anyone rushes you or changes the account at the last minute, stop.</p>
</div>

## What actually delays closings

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Slow document responses</b> The number one cause, by a wide margin. A condition that sits in your inbox for three days pushes your closing three days.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>A low appraisal</b> If the home appraises under the contract price, the gap has to be renegotiated, covered in cash, or the deal restructured. Budget a few days for that conversation.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>New credit activity</b> Financing furniture before closing triggers a re-underwrite. Buy nothing on credit until you have keys.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Unsourced deposits</b> Any deposit that isn't payroll needs a paper trail. A $3,000 transfer from a relative with no letter can hold a file for a week.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Title surprises</b> An old lien, a boundary issue, an unreleased mortgage from a previous owner. The title company usually clears these, but not overnight.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Changing jobs</b> Employment is verified again days before funding. If a job change is coming, tell your loan officer before it happens, not after.</span></div>
</div>

## Two things to do before you're even under contract

Have your [pre-approval done and your documents gathered](/blog/prequalification-vs-preapproval), and know your real cash-to-close number so the wire doesn't surprise you — that's in [the closing cost breakdown](/blog/closing-costs-explained).

Do those two things and a 30-day close stops being ambitious. It becomes the default.
