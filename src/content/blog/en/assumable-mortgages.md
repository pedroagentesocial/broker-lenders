Millions of homeowners have mortgages with rates far below today's. Most of those rates disappear when the house sells. But some can be passed along: with an **assumable mortgage**, the buyer takes over the seller's loan, with the same rate, the same balance and the same remaining term.

It sounds too good to be true. It isn't, but it comes with one large obstacle, and most articles leave it out.

## Which loans can be assumed

      <tr><td>FHA</td><td class="bl-yes">Yes</td><td>You're underwritten like any FHA borrower. On loans closed since December 15, 1989, you must plan to live there as your principal residence or a HUD-approved secondary residence</td></tr>
      <tr><td>VA</td><td class="bl-yes">Yes, and you don't have to be a veteran</td><td>The loan must be current and you must qualify on credit</td></tr>
      <tr><td>USDA</td><td>Sometimes</td><td>Needs approval from the lender and USDA, and the terms may change, so don't count on keeping the rate</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Conventional</td><td class="bl-no">Generally no</td><td>Most have a due-on-sale clause: the lender can demand the full balance when the home is sold</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

There's an easy way to check a loan: page 3 of the Loan Estimate has an "Assumption" line that says whether the lender will allow it. The seller can also ask their servicer directly.

Federal law does protect some transfers of homes with fewer than five units from a due-on-sale clause, even on conventional loans. Those include a transfer to a relative after the borrower's death, to a spouse or children, in a divorce, or into a living trust. Those are family situations, though, not a way to buy from a stranger.

## The obstacle: the equity gap

You're taking over the seller's **balance**, not the price. Everything above that balance has to be paid to the seller at closing.

The smaller the seller's balance is compared with the price, the bigger the gap. The best candidates are recent purchases made with small down payments at low rates, because little equity has built up.

## What it costs, and what to expect

Assumptions cost less than a new loan, but they aren't free. On FHA loans, the handbook caps the servicer's processing fee at $900, plus third-party costs such as the credit report. VA charges a funding fee of 0.5% of the loan, or $1,400 on $280,000, unless you're exempt. You'll also have the usual title and closing costs.

If the loan is FHA, its mortgage insurance comes with it. Expect full underwriting too: FHA assumptions must be manually underwritten.

The other cost is time. The process runs through the **seller's servicer**, not a lender you choose, and it often takes longer than a normal purchase. Ask the servicer for its process and timeline before you sign a contract, and build that into your closing date.

## The risks for the seller

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Get a formal release of liability</b> Without one, the seller can stay responsible if the buyer stops paying. FHA uses form HUD-92210.1. On a VA loan, the seller should give the holder written notice before the sale and make sure the buyer is approved.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>VA sellers: watch your entitlement</b> Generally, your VA entitlement stays tied to the loan until it's paid off, unless the buyer is an eligible veteran who substitutes their own. If you plan to use a VA loan again, settle this first.</span></div>
</div>

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>Be very careful with "subject-to" deals, where the buyer takes the home and just keeps making the seller's payments without the servicer's approval. That is not an assumption. The loan stays in the seller's name, the due-on-sale clause can still be used, and both sides are exposed. If someone offers you this, talk to a real estate attorney before you sign anything.</p>
</div>
