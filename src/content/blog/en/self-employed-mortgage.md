When you work for yourself, a lender doesn't look at what your business brings in. It looks at what your tax returns say you earned. For a lot of business owners, that's the moment the numbers stop matching: the deductions that saved you money in April are the same ones that lower the income you qualify with.

The rules below are Fannie Mae's, which cover most conventional loans. FHA, VA and USDA have their own versions, and each lender can ask for more.

## Who counts as self-employed

Fannie Mae's line is simple: if you own **25% or more** of a business, you're self-employed for that income. It doesn't matter whether you're a sole proprietor, a partner, an LLC member or a shareholder in your own corporation. If you own less than 25%, that income is reviewed under different rules.

## How much history you need

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The standard is two years</b> Fannie Mae wants a two-year history of earnings from self-employment.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Twelve months can work</b> If your most recent signed personal and business tax returns show a full 12 months of income from the current business, and before that you earned the same or more in the same line of work or a similar one.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>The lender looks at the business too</b> Your experience and how much debt the business has piled up are part of the decision.</span></div>
</div>

So a nurse who opens her own home health agency, or an electrician who goes out on his own, can often qualify after one full tax year. Someone who switches to a completely new field usually needs the full two years.

## Which tax returns you'll need

Normally, **two years of signed personal tax returns with all schedules**, plus two years of business returns if you have a partnership, S corporation or corporation. IRS transcripts for the same years can stand in for the personal returns. Two exceptions can cut that down:

      <tr><td>No business returns</td><td>Two years of personal returns only</td><td>You're paying the down payment, closing costs and reserves with personal money, you've been in the same business at least five years, and your returns show your self-employment income going up over the two years</td></tr>

  </table>
</div>

If your income is reported on Schedule C and Fannie Mae's automated system can validate it, the lender may not need the returns at all. Either way, expect to sign IRS Form 4506-C at or before closing so the lender can pull transcripts. A business owner with personal and business returns signs a separate form for each type of return.

## How your income is actually figured

The lender writes up an analysis of your income, usually on Fannie Mae's Form 1084 or a tool that follows the same method. It compares year to year how your gross income, expenses and taxable income are moving, to judge whether the business is stable. Then it starts from your taxable profit and adjusts it.

On Schedule C, the lender **adds back** expenses that didn't take cash out of your pocket: depreciation, depletion, the business use of your home, amortization and casualty losses. It **subtracts** income that won't come again, and the meals and entertainment exclusion.

## If you get a K-1

Partnerships, S corporations and LLCs taxed like either one report your share of the profit on a Schedule K-1. You pay tax on it whether or not the business paid it out to you, so the lender has to check that you can actually get to that money. It counts in one of two ways: a **steady, documented history of cash distributions** that matches the income being used, or proof the business has enough cash on hand, usually a liquidity ratio of one or more.

## Profit and loss statements

A year-to-date profit and loss statement **isn't required for most businesses**. A lender can ask for one if your application is dated more than 120 days after the end of your business's tax year and it wants support that the income is still there. If you plan to use business money for the down payment, the lender also has to confirm that taking it out won't hurt the business.
