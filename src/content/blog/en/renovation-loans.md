The cheapest house on the street usually needs work. The trouble is that a normal mortgage pays for the house, not the kitchen, and after the down payment and closing costs few buyers have $60,000 left over.

A renovation loan solves that. You borrow the purchase price and the repair budget in one mortgage, with one rate and one monthly payment. The repair money sits in an account and goes out as the work gets done.

## The three main options

  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th></th><th>FHA Limited 203(k)</th><th>FHA Standard 203(k)</th><th>Fannie Mae HomeStyle</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Best for</td><td>Minor remodeling and nonstructural repairs</td><td>Major work, including structural</td><td>Most projects, including upgrades you simply want</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Repair budget</td><td>Up to $75,000</td><td>At least $5,000; the FHA loan limit is the real ceiling</td><td>No minimum; up to 75% of the lesser of price plus renovation costs, or the as-completed value</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Time to finish</td><td>Up to 9 months</td><td>Up to 12 months</td><td>Up to 15 months from closing</td></tr>
      <tr><td>203(k) consultant</td><td>Optional</td><td class="bl-no">Required</td><td>Not part of the program</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Who can use it</td><td>Owner-occupants</td><td>Owner-occupants</td><td class="bl-yes">Principal homes, second homes and investment properties</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The $75,000 limit on the Limited 203(k) took effect for FHA case numbers assigned on or after November 4, 2024. HUD reviews it each year, so confirm the current number. Before that it was $35,000, and many websites still say so.

HomeStyle is also flexible on the type of work: it doesn't require any repairs in particular and it doesn't restrict the type of renovation, although a complete tear-down and rebuild isn't allowed. Pools and accessory units can qualify if local zoning permits them.

The appraisal is what makes this work. It's done **as if the repairs were finished**, so the lender is looking at the home's future value, not its condition today.

## How the process works

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Before you offer</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Talk to a lender that does these loans</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Not every lender offers them. Tell your agent too, and allow extra time in the contract.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Under contract</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Get the scope and the bids</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">You choose a qualified contractor, and the lender reviews their credentials and bid. On a Standard 203(k), the consultant inspects the home and writes up the work.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Before closing</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Appraisal on the finished value</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">The appraiser values the home using the plans. If that value falls short, you may have to cut the scope or bring more cash.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">4</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">After closing</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">The work happens and the money follows</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">The repair funds are held and released as work is completed and verified. The contractor doesn't get it all up front.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">5</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">At the end</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Final inspection</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Completion is confirmed and the loan becomes an ordinary mortgage.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

## What people get wrong

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Assuming you'll do the work yourself</b> It's limited. HomeStyle caps do-it-yourself work at 10% of the as-completed value, needs the lender's approval, and your labor can't be counted or reimbursed. FHA has its own self-help rules.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Forgetting where you'll live</b> If the home can't be lived in during the work, you may be paying rent and a mortgage. Both programs can finance some payments: up to 12 months on a Standard 203(k), and up to six on HomeStyle for a principal residence.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Picking Limited for a big job</b> Limited can't be used for major work. If the job needs more than nine months, it won't fit.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Budgeting with no cushion</b> Old houses hide things. Plan for surprises in both the budget and the timeline, and ask your lender how much contingency your loan requires.</span></div>
</div>

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>A renovation loan isn't the right tool for every repair. If the job is small and you have the savings, a normal loan closes faster and with less paperwork. If you already own the home and have a low rate, a <a href="/blog/heloc-vs-cash-out-refinance">HELOC</a> may make more sense than replacing the whole mortgage. These loans shine when the house is a good deal because of its condition, and when you couldn't otherwise pay for the work.</p>
</div>
