Every mortgage ad and every Loan Estimate shows two percentages side by side, and the second one is always a little higher. That second number, the APR, was created so you could compare loans that have different rates and different fees. It does that well, as long as you know the one assumption hidden inside it.

## Two numbers, two jobs

If two loans have the same rate but one has $4,000 more in lender fees, the payments are identical and only the APR shows the difference.

## What goes into the APR, and what doesn't

The APR is built from what federal rules call the **finance charge**. In a home loan, that means the costs of the credit itself, not every cost of buying the house.

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Included: interest</b> The rate itself, over the full term.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Included: points and loan fees</b> Discount points, origination and underwriting fees, and mortgage broker fees.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Included: required mortgage insurance</b> Conventional PMI and FHA's mortgage insurance premiums count, so a loan with mortgage insurance shows a bigger gap between rate and APR.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Generally left out</b> Title insurance and title search, the appraisal, the credit report, document preparation, notary fees and the money you put into escrow for taxes and insurance.</span></div>
</div>

That second list matters. Title and appraisal costs can add up to thousands of dollars and never appear in the APR. Two lenders can have nearly the same APR and very different cash to close. Look at the APR and the full Loan Estimate together.

## An example: two offers on the same loan

Say you're borrowing $400,000 on a 30-year fixed loan and you get two offers. Both charge $1,500 in lender fees. Offer A lowers the rate by charging one point, $4,000.

So the APR answers one question well: which loan is cheaper if you keep it to the end. If you think you'll move or refinance in a few years, the loan with fewer upfront costs can win even with a higher APR. The CFPB's advice is to ask for Loan Estimates for the **same kind of loan** from different lenders, on the same day if you can, since rates change daily. Comparing a 30-year fixed with a 15-year or an FHA loan with a conventional one through the APR alone mixes up different products.

## When the APR can mislead you

- **Adjustable-rate loans.** The CFPB points out that the APR on an ARM doesn't reflect the maximum rate the loan could reach. Compare an ARM's APR with a fixed loan's APR carefully, and read the adjustment table on the Loan Estimate.
- **Home equity lines of credit.** A HELOC's APR generally doesn't include fees, while a mortgage's does, so the two aren't directly comparable.
- **Mortgage insurance.** An FHA loan's APR includes its mortgage insurance premiums. That's useful when you compare it with a conventional loan. Our guide to [mortgage insurance](/blog/mortgage-insurance-explained/) explains when each kind can come off, which the APR alone won't tell you.
- **Third-party costs.** As above, title and appraisal fees aren't in the APR. A lender with a low APR can still send you to closing with more cash due.

## If the APR changes before closing

You have to receive your Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing. Federal rules treat a mortgage APR as accurate if it's within 1/8 of a percentage point of the actual APR, or 1/4 of a point on some irregular loans. If the APR goes up beyond that after you've received the Closing Disclosure, the lender has to give you a corrected one and a new three-business-day waiting period before you can close. A change in the loan product or a new prepayment penalty also restarts the clock.
