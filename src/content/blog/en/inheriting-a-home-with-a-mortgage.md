When someone you love dies, the mortgage on their house is usually the last thing you want to think about. But the payment keeps coming due, and many families get bad advice in those first weeks: that the bank will take the house, or that the heir has to refinance right away.

Neither is true in most cases. Federal law protects relatives who inherit a home, and federal servicing rules tell the mortgage company how to treat you.

## The mortgage doesn't disappear, and the lender can't call it due just because of the death

Most mortgages have a "due-on-sale" clause: if the home changes hands, the lender can demand the full balance. A federal law, the Garn-St Germain Act, takes that option away for certain transfers of a home with fewer than five units. Among them:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A transfer to a relative because the borrower died.</b> An adult child who inherits a parent's home is the most common case.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A transfer on the death of a joint tenant</b> or tenant by the entirety, when the surviving owner keeps the home.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A transfer where the borrower's spouse or children become owners.</b></span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>A transfer into a living trust</b> where the borrower is and stays a beneficiary.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>The protection is against the lender demanding payment, not against missed payments.</b> The loan still has to be paid on time. If it falls behind, the lender can foreclose like with any other loan.</span></div>
</div>

In practice, that means the existing loan can stay in place with the **same rate, balance and terms**. If your parent locked in a low rate years ago, that rate can stay with the house.

## Getting the servicer to recognize you

Federal rules call a person who inherits the home this way a **successor in interest**. The servicer, the company that collects the payments, has specific duties once it learns about the death:

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">You tell the servicer</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Notify them in writing</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Let the servicer know the borrower died and that you are, or may be, the new owner. Use the address on the monthly statement and keep a copy.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">The servicer responds</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">It tells you which documents it needs</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">It has to promptly decide what it reasonably needs to confirm your identity and ownership, and tell you. Often that's a death certificate plus a copy of the will or a letter from the executor. What you can get depends on the state.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">After you send them</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">It confirms you, or says what's missing</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">It has to promptly make a decision and tell you whether you're confirmed, which documents are still missing, or that it decided you aren't a successor.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

Once you're a **confirmed successor in interest**, the servicing rules treat you as a borrower. That means you can request information about the account, the escrow rules apply to you, you can send notices of error, and you can be evaluated for help if you're struggling to pay. You don't have to wait until you're officially on the loan.

## You usually don't have to refinance

A 2024 CFPB report found that some mortgage companies push heirs to refinance into a new loan, often at a higher rate, instead of letting them keep the existing one. That's often unnecessary. The CFPB has also said that adding a confirmed heir as a borrower on the existing loan doesn't trigger the ability-to-repay rules that apply to a new mortgage.

For loans owned by Fannie Mae, the servicer has to process a transfer to a relative of a deceased borrower without reviewing or approving the terms of the transfer. For loans Fannie Mae bought before June 1, 2016, the relative also has to live in the home for the transfer to qualify.

When several siblings inherit together and one wants to keep the house, buying the others out usually takes new money, which often means a refinance. Run the numbers on the existing rate versus a new one before deciding. [Assumable mortgages](/blog/assumable-mortgages/) explains how the gap between a home's value and the loan balance works.

## If it was a reverse mortgage

Reverse mortgages work differently. When the last borrower dies, the loan generally becomes due, unless there's an eligible non-borrowing spouse who qualifies to stay. To keep the home, heirs have to pay off the loan. To sell it when the home is worth less than the balance, heirs can pay the full balance or 95% of the appraised value, whichever is less, and FHA insurance covers the rest. Heirs have 30 days after the due-and-payable notice, and the CFPB says that can sometimes be extended up to six months. [Reverse mortgages, explained](/blog/reverse-mortgages-explained/) covers the details.
