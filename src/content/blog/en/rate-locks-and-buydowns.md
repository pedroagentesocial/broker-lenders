There are three separate tools people confuse constantly: locking a rate, buying points, and a temporary buydown. They cost different amounts, they help in different situations, and only one of them is free.

## 1. The rate lock — free, and it has a clock

A lock is the lender's commitment to honor a specific rate for a specific number of days. It costs nothing up front, and it exists because rates move daily while your loan takes weeks.

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th>Situation</th><th>What it means</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Floating</td><td>No commitment. Your rate is whatever the market gives you on the day you lock. Fine early, dangerous late.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>30–60 day lock</td><td>The normal range for a purchase. Should comfortably cover a standard escrow.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Lock expires before closing</td><td>You pay to extend — typically a fraction of a point per week — or you're back at current market rates. Extensions are common and are usually the smaller cost.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Float-down option</td><td>Some lenders let you re-lock lower once if the market drops meaningfully. It's rarely free and the trigger threshold matters more than the feature.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Rates drop a lot after you lock</td><td>Ask. Not every lender will renegotiate, but many will rather than lose the loan.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

You generally can't lock without a property address, which is why a pre-approval is not a locked rate. The moment to lock is when you're under contract and your closing date is real.

## 2. Discount points — buying the rate down permanently

One point costs 1% of the loan amount and typically lowers the rate by around a quarter percent. Here's what that actually buys.

  <p class="bl-fig-title">One point on a $388,000 loan</p>
  <p class="bl-fig-sub">Illustrative pricing: 6.50% with no points, 6.25% with one point.</p>
  <div class="bl-stats">
    <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">$3,880</div><div class="bl-stat-label">What the point costs at closing</div></div>
    <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">$64</div><div class="bl-stat-label">What it saves you every month</div></div>
    <div class="bl-stat is-accent"><div class="bl-stat-num">61 months</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Break-even — about 5 years</div></div>
  </div>
  <p class="bl-fig-note">Below five years, you lost money. Above five years, every month is profit. So the only question that matters is: how confident are you that you'll still have <em>this exact loan</em> in five years — not this house, this loan. A refinance resets the clock and the point is gone.</p>
</figure>

That framing kills most point purchases, and it should. If rates are historically high, you're probably refinancing within five years, and paying to permanently lower a rate you plan to replace is the wrong trade. When rates are low and you'll hold the loan, points get much more attractive.

## 3. The temporary buydown — cheap payments early

A 2-1 buydown doesn't change your loan. It puts money in an escrow account that subsidizes your payment for the first two years: two points below the note rate in year one, one point below in year two, then the real rate forever.

  <p class="bl-fig-note">Two things people miss. First, you are qualified at 6.50%, not 4.50% — the underwriter never sees the discounted payment, so this doesn't help you afford more house. </figure>

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>Almost every buydown I write is paid by the seller, not the buyer. It's part of the seller's closing-cost contribution, and on a house that's been listed for sixty days it's an easier ask than a price cut — and it does more for the buyer. $8,844 in buydown funds beats $8,844 off the price, which would only lower the payment by about $56 a month. Same money, four times the effect in the years that matter most.</p>
</div>

## Which one is your problem?

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>"I'm worried rates rise before I close"</b> That's a lock. Free, and you should have one the week you go under contract.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>"The payment is tight for the next two years"</b> That's a buydown — and ask the seller to fund it.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>"This is my forever house and rates are low"</b> That's points. Run the break-even, and be honest about the horizon.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>"I want a lower advertised rate"</b> Careful. A rate quoted with points isn't comparable to one without. Compare Loan Estimates on total cost, not the headline number.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>"I'll buy points to qualify"</b> Points do lower the qualifying payment, but spending your reserves to squeeze into a house is how people end up house-poor. Reserves first.</span></div>
</div>
