The first question almost everybody asks me is *"what price should I be looking at?"* — and it's the wrong question. Price is the last thing that gets decided. Before price, an underwriter looks at three numbers, in this order, and those three numbers set your ceiling.

Here they are.

## Number 1: your debt-to-income ratio

Your debt-to-income ratio — DTI — is every monthly debt payment divided by your **gross** (pre-tax) monthly income. The CFPB defines it exactly that way, and every loan program in the country runs on some version of it.

Two things surprise people:

- It uses **gross** income, not what lands in your account.
- It counts the *minimum* payment on your debts — credit cards, car loans, student loans, child support — plus the **new** housing payment, including taxes and insurance.

Your groceries, your gas, your phone bill and your daycare are not in it. That's why the number a lender approves you for and the number you should actually spend are rarely the same. More on that in number 3.

### What the programs actually allow

The old "43% is the hard limit" line you'll still read online came from the original Qualified Mortgage rule and no longer describes how loans are underwritten. Here is where the real ceilings sit today:

Two borrowers with identical income can get very different answers here. A 780 credit score with six months of reserves gets pushed toward the top of that range. A 620 score with $800 in the bank does not.

## Number 2: the cash you actually need

This is where most first-time buyers get caught. They save the down payment and nothing else. The down payment is usually the *smaller* half of what you bring to the table.

Two things move this number in your favor, and both are negotiable:

- **Seller credits.** In a market where a home has been sitting, asking the seller to cover $8,000 of closing costs is often easier than asking for $8,000 off the price — and it helps you more, because it's cash you keep.
- **Down payment assistance.** Every state I'm licensed in has programs for first-time buyers, and "first-time" usually just means you haven't owned in three years. They're underused because people assume they won't qualify.

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>I'd rather see a client buy $40,000 less house and close with three months of reserves in the bank than stretch to the maximum and close with nothing. The first water heater always breaks in year one. Every single time.</p>
</div>

## Number 3: the payment you'll be happy with

The lender's job is to decide what you *can* pay. Your job is to decide what you *want* to pay. Those are different jobs, and only one of them is protecting your weekends.

  <p class="bl-fig-note">Every $500 a month of other debt moves roughly $75,000 to $85,000 of purchase price out of reach, depending on where rates sit. That car is not a $500 decision — it's a bedroom.</p>
</figure>

This is also the most fixable of the three numbers. Paying off a car with four payments left, or moving a credit card balance below its limit, can change your approval in a single credit refresh. I've had clients gain $60,000 of buying power in ten days by closing out two small balances.
