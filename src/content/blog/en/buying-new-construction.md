A brand-new home has a lot going for it: nobody else's repairs, modern systems, and often a builder willing to help with costs. It also works differently from buying a resale home. The contract is usually the builder's own, the timeline is longer, and the best incentive is often tied to a lender the builder picks.

None of that is bad. It just means you should compare the offer and read the contract before you sign.

This guide is about the first two, where you buy from a builder and close once the house is done.

## The preferred-lender incentive

Builders often offer something if you finance with their preferred or affiliated lender: a credit toward closing costs, a rate buydown, or upgrades. That can be a good deal. It can also be a reason to stop comparing.

<div class="bl-versus">
  <div class="bl-versus-card is-a">
    <p class="bl-versus-head">The builder's lender</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">"$10,000 toward closing costs"</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>Look at the rate and points on the Loan Estimate, not just the credit</li>
      <li>Check whether the credit depends on a specific rate or loan type</li>
      <li>Ask what happens to the credit if closing is delayed</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-versus-card is-b">
    <p class="bl-versus-head">Another lender</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">No builder credit</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>The rate or fees may be lower enough to make up the difference</li>
      <li>Ask the builder if they'll offer part of the incentive as a price reduction instead</li>
      <li>Compare the total cost over the years you expect to keep the loan</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

The fair comparison is simple: get a **Loan Estimate** from each lender for the same loan type, on the same day, and compare the rate, the cash to close and the monthly payment. If the builder's package still wins, great. You'll know it, instead of assuming it.

What the federal rules say: if the builder refers you to a lender it has an ownership or affiliate relationship with, it has to give you a written disclosure of that relationship, and it can't require you to use that lender. Incentives to choose it are common, though. And any credit from the builder counts as an interested party contribution, which has limits by loan type. [Closing costs, line by line](/blog/closing-costs-explained/) shows those limits.

## Rate locks when the house isn't built yet

A normal lock lasts 30 to 60 days. A build-to-order home can take several months, so you have two choices:

  <div class="bl-step">

    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Option A</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Float until the closing date is close</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">You lock once the builder gives a reliable completion date. No extra cost, but if rates rise in the meantime, you get the higher rate.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Option B</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">An extended lock</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Some lenders offer longer locks for new construction. They usually cost more, through a higher rate or a fee, and the terms vary. Ask what the lock costs, how long it lasts and what happens if the build runs late.</p>
    </div>
  </div>

Build dates slip often. Weather, inspections and supply problems are common reasons. Plan your lease or the sale of your current home with some cushion. [Rate locks, points and 2-1 buydowns](/blog/rate-locks-and-buydowns/) explains how locks and extensions work.

## The property tax surprise

Your first escrow estimate is often based on the tax bill for the lot, or for a partly built house. In Utah, property is valued as of January 1 each year. If your home was a lot or half-finished on January 1, the first bill can be much lower than the ones that follow. Then the next escrow analysis catches up, and your payment goes up.

Ask your lender to estimate taxes on the finished home, not on the lot. In Utah, also ask the county about the primary residential exemption: for a home under construction, there's a form (PT-24) to declare that it will be your primary residence. [What's inside your mortgage payment](/blog/inside-your-monthly-payment/) explains how escrow shortages work.
