Most people hear "USDA loan" and picture a farm. In practice it's a home loan for regular houses in small towns and on the edges of growing metro areas, and it's one of only two common mortgages that let you buy with **no down payment at all**. The other is the VA loan, which requires military service.

The catch is that two things have to qualify: the home's location and your household's income.

## The two tests

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>The home is in an eligible area.</b> USDA publishes a map. Many towns outside the core of a metro area qualify, and so do some neighborhoods you might not think of as rural. Don't guess: look up the exact address.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Your household income is under the limit.</b> The program is for moderate-income households, and the limit depends on the county and on how many people live in the home. It counts the income of everyone in the household, not only the people on the loan.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>You'll live there.</b> It has to be your primary residence. USDA doesn't guarantee loans for investment properties or vacation homes.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>The household income test surprises people.</b> An adult child or a parent who lives with you and works can push the household over the limit, even if they aren't borrowing. Some deductions apply, such as for dependents, so run the numbers before you decide.</span></div>
</div>

You can check both the address and the income limit on USDA's [eligibility site](https://eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov/). The map and the limits change from time to time, so check again if you looked months ago.

## What it costs: the two fees

A USDA loan has no monthly mortgage insurance in the usual sense, but it has two fees that work the same way. For the current fiscal year, USDA has kept them at **1% upfront** and **0.35% per year**.

  <p class="bl-fig-sub">Illustrative. The annual fee is based on the loan balance, so it drops slowly as you pay the loan down.</p>

  </div>
  <p class="bl-fig-note">Most buyers finance the upfront fee instead of paying it in cash, which means paying interest on it too. The annual fee lasts for the life of the loan.</p>

That last point matters. On a conventional loan, mortgage insurance comes off once you have enough equity. The USDA annual fee doesn't. If you expect to stay in the home a long time, compare the total cost against a conventional loan with a small down payment. [Mortgage insurance, explained](/blog/mortgage-insurance-explained/) puts all the loan types side by side.

## How much payment you can carry

USDA's rule for the guaranteed program looks at two ratios of your monthly income before taxes:

<figure class="bl-fig">
  <p class="bl-fig-title">The 29/41 guideline on a $6,000 monthly income</p>
  <p class="bl-fig-sub">House payment includes principal, interest, taxes, insurance and the annual fee.</p>
  <div class="bl-bars">
    <div class="bl-bar"><div class="bl-bar-label">House payment <small>29% of income</small></div><div class="bl-bar-track"><div class="bl-bar-fill" style="--w:71%;--c:#dc2626">$1,740</div></div></div>
    <div class="bl-bar"><div class="bl-bar-label">House payment plus all other debts <small>41% of income</small></div><div class="bl-bar-track"><div class="bl-bar-fill" style="--w:100%;--c:#111827">$2,460</div></div></div>
  </div>
  <p class="bl-fig-note">Applications above these ratios can sometimes be approved with strong compensating factors, such as savings or a long, stable job history. That's decided case by case.</p>
</figure>

There's no maximum loan amount like the FHA or conforming limits. In practice the income limit and the 29/41 ratios cap how much you can borrow. Lenders also set their own credit score minimums on top of USDA's requirement of a credit history that shows you pay your debts.

USDA usually wins when the home qualifies, your income fits and your savings are thin. If you have 5% or more to put down and good credit, a conventional loan is often cheaper over time. If the home doesn't qualify, USDA isn't an option no matter how good the file is. [Conventional, FHA, VA, USDA or jumbo](/blog/loan-types-compared/) walks through the whole decision.

## Two programs with the same name

The loan most buyers use is the **guaranteed** loan: a private lender like us makes it, and USDA guarantees it. USDA also runs a **direct** loan program for lower-income households, where USDA itself is the lender. It has its own income limits, its own application and its own waiting times, and it goes through your local Rural Development office, not through a mortgage lender.
