Getting a second or third mortgage offer is one of the few things in a home purchase that costs you almost nothing and can save you real money. The hard part isn't asking. It's making sure the offers you get back can actually be compared, because two lenders quoting different loans on different days will always look different, even when neither is a better deal.

## Every lender uses the same form

Since 2015, nearly every home loan comes with a **Loan Estimate**, a three-page form that every lender has to fill out the same way. The CFPB designed it so you can put offers side by side: same sections, same line names, same order. A "fee sheet," a "worksheet" or a rate quote in an email isn't a Loan Estimate, and it doesn't carry the same rules.

## What it takes to get one, and what it can't cost you

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Six pieces of information</b> Your name, your income, your Social Security number so the lender can pull credit, the property address, an estimate of its value and the loan amount you want. Once the lender has those, you've applied.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Three business days</b> From that moment the lender has three business days to give you the Loan Estimate.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Only a credit report fee</b> Until you've received the Loan Estimate and told the lender you want to go ahead, no one can charge you a fee except a reasonable charge for pulling your credit report. No application fee, no appraisal deposit.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>No paperwork required first</b> The lender can't make you send pay stubs, bank statements or other documents before it gives you the Loan Estimate. It can still ask you questions.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Saying nothing isn't saying yes</b> You choose a lender by telling it you intend to proceed. Under the rule, your silence doesn't count as that.</span></div>
</div>

If you don't have an address yet, you haven't technically applied, but most lenders will still run numbers for a sample property at your price. That's useful for planning. Just remember that the binding comparison starts once there's a real address.

## Your credit score can handle it

People skip the second quote because they think every credit check knocks their score down. The CFPB is clear on this: within a **45-day window**, multiple credit checks from mortgage lenders are recorded on your credit report as a single inquiry, and the impact is the same no matter how many lenders you talk to. Do your shopping in a tight period and it costs you nothing extra.

## Make the offers comparable before you compare them

Rates move every day, sometimes more than once. A quote from Monday and a quote from Thursday can differ just because the market moved. Ask each lender for:

## Where the lender's price lives

On page 2, the closing costs are split into sections, and they don't all mean the same thing when you're comparing.

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th>Section</th><th>What's in it</th><th>How to read it when comparing</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>A. Origination Charges</td><td>The lender's own fees and any points</td><td>This is the lender's price. Compare it line by line.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>B. Services You Cannot Shop For</td><td>Appraisal, credit report, flood certification and others the lender picks</td><td>Third-party costs. They tend to be close between lenders.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>C. Services You Can Shop For</td><td>Title and settlement services, survey and similar</td><td>Mostly estimates of someone else's bill. A low number here isn't a saving if it was just estimated low.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Lender Credits (in J)</td><td>Money the lender gives you toward closing costs</td><td>Usually comes with a higher rate. Compare it together with the rate.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>F, G and H</td><td>Prepaid interest, insurance, escrow deposits and other costs</td><td>Driven by your taxes, insurance and closing date, not the lender. Ignore differences here.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

Page 1 matters too. Check that the **Loan Terms** show the same answers on each offer about prepayment penalties and balloon payments, and look at the rate lock line at the top: it tells you whether the rate is locked and the date the other estimated closing costs expire. Page 3 has the APR and the "In 5 Years" figure, which we explain in [APR vs. interest rate](/blog/apr-vs-interest-rate/).

## An example: the lower rate isn't free

Two Loan Estimates for the same $400,000, 30-year fixed loan, requested the same day with the same lock. Illustrative numbers.

Offer B saves about $33 a month but costs $2,000 more at closing. It takes about 60 months, five years, for the lower payment to pay back the point. If you expect to sell or refinance before then, Offer A is cheaper even though its rate is higher. If you'll keep the loan for a long time, B wins. The point of comparing isn't to find the lowest rate. It's to find the right mix of rate and cost for how long you'll keep the loan. [Rate locks, points and 2-1 buydowns](/blog/rate-locks-and-buydowns/) goes deeper on points.

## Once you've chosen

Tell the lender you've chosen it, in writing if you can. That's your intent to proceed, and only then can the lender start charging fees like the appraisal. Timing matters: if you take longer than **10 business days** after receiving the Loan Estimate to say yes, the lender is allowed to give you a new estimate with different numbers, no reason needed. And if the rate isn't locked, it can change at any time until you lock it.

You can also use one offer to negotiate with another lender. Lenders know you have the same form in hand and can see exactly where they're higher.
