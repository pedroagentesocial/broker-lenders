When you already own a home, buying the next one comes with two problems first-time buyers don't have. Your down payment is probably sitting in the equity of the house you live in. And until that house sells, the lender may count its payment on top of the new one.

Neither problem is a dead end. But the order of steps decides how much you qualify for, and the rules are specific.

## Problem one: two payments in your debt ratio

Under Fannie Mae's rules, if your current home hasn't sold by the time you close on the new one, the lender has to qualify you with **both** payments: the current one and the new one, each with taxes, insurance and any HOA dues.

There's one exception. If your current home is under contract but won't close before your new loan, the lender doesn't have to count the old payment if it has **the signed sales contract** and **confirmation that the buyer's financing contingencies have been cleared**.

## Problem two: your down payment is in the old house

There are three common ways to get to that money before the sale closes.

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Anticipated sale proceeds</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Qualify on the money the sale will bring</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">If your home is listed but not sold, Fannie Mae lets the lender qualify you on the expected proceeds. With a sales price, it's the price minus selling costs and all liens. Without one, it's 90% of the list price minus all liens. The lender still has to document what you actually receive, usually with the settlement statement from your sale.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Bridge loan</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">A short-term loan against the old house</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Fannie Mae accepts a bridge loan as a source of funds. It can't be cross-collateralized against the new home, and the lender has to document that you can carry the new home, the old home, the bridge loan and your other debts. Fannie Mae doesn't set a maximum term.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">HELOC</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">A line of credit on your current home</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Open it while you still live there and have a steady payment history. The payment on the line counts in your debt ratio, so it only helps if you can qualify with it.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

Bridge loans and HELOCs both mean carrying more debt for a while, and both get paid off when the old house sells. If the sale takes longer than planned, you're the one paying for it. [HELOC or cash-out refinance?](/blog/heloc-vs-cash-out-refinance/) explains how a line of credit works.

## Keeping the old home as a rental

Many owners with a low rate on their current home would rather keep it and rent it out. The expected rent can help you qualify for the new loan, but less than you'd think, and the rules depend on the loan.

<div class="bl-versus">
  <div class="bl-versus-card is-a">
    <p class="bl-versus-head">Conventional (Fannie Mae)</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">75% of market rent</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>The rent is supported with an appraisal showing market rent, a rent schedule, or a market analysis with at least three comparable rentals. A lease can't be used for a departing residence</li>
      <li>The lender takes 75% of the rent and subtracts the full payment on the old home</li>
      <li>If the result is positive, it can only offset that payment. If it's negative, it's added to your debts</li>
      <li>With less than 12 months of property management experience, you need 6 months of the old home's payment in reserves, on top of any other reserves required</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-versus-card is-b">
    <p class="bl-versus-head">FHA</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">Only if you move 100+ miles</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>Rent from the home you're leaving counts only if you're relocating more than 100 miles away</li>
      <li>The lender needs a lease of at least one year after closing, and proof the tenant paid the security deposit or first month's rent</li>
      <li>Without a rental history, it also needs an appraisal showing market rent and at least 25% equity in the home</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Here's the Fannie Mae math with real numbers. Market rent of $2,400 times 75% is $1,800. The old home's payment is $2,100, so the result is negative $300. That $300 a month goes into your debt ratio. Renting the house helps a lot compared with counting the full $2,100, but it doesn't make the old payment disappear.

Keeping the home also changes how it's taxed and insured. [Second home or investment property?](/blog/second-home-vs-investment-property/) explains what changes when a home becomes a rental.

## Buying subject to the sale of your home in Utah

The Utah purchase contract has a box for a **Subject to Sale of Buyer's Property** condition: your purchase depends on the proceeds from selling the home you already own. If you check it, you have to attach the addendum that sets the terms. If no addendum is attached, the condition doesn't apply, even if the box is checked.

Sellers often prefer offers without that condition, especially in a busy market. That's why it's worth knowing before you write an offer which of the options above you qualify for without it.
