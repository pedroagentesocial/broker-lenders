When you apply for a mortgage, one of the first questions is how you'll use the home. It looks like a formality, but your answer sets the down payment, the rate, the cash you need in reserve, and whether rent can help you qualify.

There are three possible answers: **principal residence**, **second home** or **investment property**. Each one comes with its own rules.

Reserves are payments you have saved after closing. If you own other financed properties, the lender adds a percentage of their mortgage balances on top: 2% for one to four financed properties, 4% for five or six, and 6% for seven to ten.

## What makes a home a "second home"

According to Fannie Mae, a second home must:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Be a one-unit home</b> A duplex can't be a second home. It can be a principal residence or an investment.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Be used by you for part of the year</b> And be suitable for living in year-round.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Be under your exclusive control</b> No timeshares, and no agreement that gives a management company control over who stays there.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check is-warn"><span class="bl-check-icon">!</span><span><b>Not be primarily a rental</b> You can rent it occasionally, but that rent can't be used to qualify you for the loan.</span></div>
</div>

A detail many people get wrong: Fannie Mae's occupancy rules **don't set a minimum distance** from your principal residence. Lenders still look at whether it makes sense as a second home. A condo at the beach is the classic example, while a house down the street with tenants is not.

## Investment properties: rent helps, but not automatically

The advantage of an investment property is that its rent can count as income. Lenders don't count every dollar of it, though, and Fannie Mae adds a condition that surprises many first-time investors: you can only use **positive** rental income to qualify if you have at least 12 months of property management experience. Without it, the rent can only offset the payment on the property itself.

In practice, buying your first rental means qualifying mostly on your own income, keeping six months of reserves and putting at least 15% down.

## The strategy worth knowing: buy 2 to 4 units and live in one

If you want rental income but don't have 15% to 25% to put down, a home with two to four units that you live in is treated as a **principal residence**. That opens the lower down payments:

    <p class="bl-versus-head">FHA</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">3.5% down on up to 4 units</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>You must live in one of the units</li>
      <li>On 3–4 units, a "self-sufficiency" test applies: the property's net rental income has to cover the full payment</li>
      <li>Requires FHA mortgage insurance</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-versus-card is-b">
    <p class="bl-versus-head">Conventional</p>
    <p class="bl-versus-big">5% down on 2–4 units</p>
    <ul class="bl-versus-list">
      <li>Since late 2023, Fannie Mae allows up to 95% financing on owner-occupied 2–4 unit homes with automated underwriting</li>
      <li>Plan to move in within 60 days of closing and live there at least a year</li>
      <li>After that, you can move out and keep it as a rental</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

A 2–4 unit home is more work than a single-family house: tenants, repairs and vacancies. But for many people it's the most affordable way to own their first rental.
