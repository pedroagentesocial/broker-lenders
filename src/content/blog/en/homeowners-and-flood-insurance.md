Insurance is the part of the home purchase people leave for last. Then, a week before closing, the lender asks for a policy, the quote comes in higher than expected, or the home turns out to be in a flood zone.

Your lender requires insurance because the house is the loan's collateral. If the house burns down, the debt doesn't go away, so the lender wants the house to be rebuildable. Here's what that means for you.

## What the policy must cover

On a conventional loan, Fannie Mae requires the home to be insured on a **replacement cost** basis, meaning the cost to rebuild it, not its market value. Roofs are the exception and don't have to be covered at replacement cost. The policy must include these perils:

<div class="bl-checks">
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Fire and lightning</b> Plus explosion and smoke.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Windstorm and hail</b> Including named storms. If your main policy excludes them, you'll need a separate policy that covers them.</span></div>
  <div class="bl-check"><span class="bl-check-icon">✓</span><span><b>Aircraft, vehicles, riot or civil commotion</b> The rest of the required list.</span></div>
</div>

## The deductible: up to 5% is allowed

Fannie Mae allows a maximum deductible of **5% of the coverage amount**. If the policy has a separate deductible for something like windstorms, each one can be up to 5%.

<div class="bl-stats">
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">$350,000</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Coverage on the home</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat"><div class="bl-stat-num">5%</div><div class="bl-stat-label">Maximum deductible allowed</div></div>
  <div class="bl-stat is-accent"><div class="bl-stat-num">$17,500</div><div class="bl-stat-label">What you could pay out of pocket before insurance pays</div></div>
</div>

A high deductible lowers the premium, but make sure you could cover it. Many policies in storm areas use a percentage-based wind deductible, so read that line before you sign.

## When flood insurance is mandatory

If the home is in a **Special Flood Hazard Area**, which is any zone starting with "A" or "V" on FEMA's maps, the lender will require flood insurance. Check a property's zone before you make an offer, using FEMA's flood maps or by asking your agent.

The coverage required is the **lowest** of three amounts:

<div class="bl-table-wrap">
  <table class="bl-table">
    <thead><tr><th>Option</th><th>Example: a $400,000 home with a $380,000 loan</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>The full replacement cost of the building</td><td>$330,000</td></tr>
      <tr><td>The loan balance</td><td>$380,000</td></tr>
      <tr><td>The maximum available from the National Flood Insurance Program</td><td class="bl-yes">$250,000 for a single-family home ← the requirement</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The NFIP covers up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for contents on a single-family home. Contents coverage is separate and usually optional for the lender, but it's your furniture and belongings.

Timing matters. An NFIP policy usually has a **30-day waiting period** before it takes effect. If you buy it as part of getting your mortgage, there's no wait: coverage starts when the loan closes. But if you're buying flood insurance later, after you've moved in, the 30 days apply.

<div class="bl-callout">
  <p class="bl-callout-title">Pedro's note</p>
  <p>Get an insurance quote during your inspection period, not the week before closing. In some areas premiums have gone up a lot, and a quote can change your budget as much as the rate does. If the quote is too high, you'll still have time to negotiate or walk away. And if the home is in a flood zone, add that premium to your monthly budget from the start.</p>
</div>

## Your premium is part of your payment

If your loan has an escrow account, the premium is paid from it, and it's built into your monthly payment. When the premium goes up at renewal, your mortgage payment goes up too. It's one of the main reasons payments rise after the first year. You can see how that works in [What's actually inside your mortgage payment](/blog/inside-your-monthly-payment).

## Force-placed insurance: the policy you didn't choose

If your coverage lapses or the servicer can't confirm you have it, the servicer can buy a policy for you and charge you for it. It's called **force-placed insurance**, and it's usually more expensive than a policy you'd choose and may protect mostly the lender's interest. Federal rules give you time to fix it:

<div class="bl-steps">
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">1</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">At least 45 days before charging you</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">First written notice</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">The servicer has to tell you it doesn't have proof of your coverage.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step">
    <div class="bl-step-num">2</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">At least 15 days before charging you</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Reminder notice</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Sent no earlier than 30 days after the first one.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bl-step is-accent">
    <div class="bl-step-num">3</div>
    <div class="bl-step-body">
      <p class="bl-step-meta">Within 15 days after you show proof</p>
      <p class="bl-step-title">Cancellation and refund</p>
      <p class="bl-step-text">Once you show you had coverage, the servicer must cancel its policy and refund charges for any period when you already had coverage.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

Don't ignore any letter about insurance from your servicer. Send your declarations page right away.
